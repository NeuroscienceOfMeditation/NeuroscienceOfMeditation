Most summaries of swara yoga say something like: the dominant nostril changes every couple of hours. That is vague enough to be unfalsifiable. You could watch yourself for a week and never be able to say whether the claim had failed.

The text itself is not vague. The *Śiva Svarodaya* counts in a named unit, gives numbers, and divides the period into sub-phases. That specificity is the interesting part, because a claim with numbers in it can be wrong in a way a claim without them cannot.

So here is what the text actually says about the hour. The short version is that it says three different things, and does not reconcile them.

## The unit it counts in

A *ghaṭikā* is one sixtieth of a day and night — twenty-four minutes. Sixty of them make a full nychthemeron. It was measured with a water clock: a copper bowl with a hole in it that filled and sank sixty times between one sunrise and the next.

This matters because every timing claim in the text is expressed in *ghaṭikās*, and the conversions are where most popular accounts go wrong.

## Claim one: five phases inside one flow

<div class="verse">
  <p class="dev" lang="sa">प्रथमं वहते वायुर्द्वितीयं च तथानलः। तृतीयं वहते भूमिश्चतुर्थं वरुणो वहेत्॥७१॥ सार्धद्विघटिके पञ्च क्रमेणैवोदयन्ति च। क्रमादेकैकनाड्यां च तत्त्वानां पृथगुद्भवः॥७२॥</p>
  <p class="iast" lang="sa-Latn">prathamaṃ vahate vāyur dvitīyaṃ ca tathānalaḥ | tṛtīyaṃ vahate bhūmiś caturthaṃ varuṇo vahet ||71|| sārdhadvighaṭike pañca krameṇaivodayanti ca | kramād ekaikanāḍyāṃ ca tattvānāṃ pṛthag udbhavaḥ ||72||</p>
  <p class="tr">First air flows, then fire, third earth, and fourth water. The five arise in sequence within two and a half <em>ghaṭikās</em>, each element arising separately in each channel in turn. <span class="ref">— Verses 71–72</span></p>
</div>

Two and a half *ghaṭikās* is sixty minutes. On the plain reading, five elemental phases arise inside that hour — twelve minutes each — and the hour is the dominance period.

But other passages in the text attach the two and a half *ghaṭikās* to each element in turn rather than to the set, which stretches the dominance period considerably. Manuscript traditions resolve this differently. The recension I work from does not resolve it at all.

## Claim two: twelve changes in a day and night

<div class="verse">
  <p class="dev" lang="sa">अहोरात्रस्य मध्ये तु ज्ञेया द्वादश संक्रमाः। वृषकर्कटकन्यालिमृगमीना निशाकरे॥७३॥</p>
  <p class="iast" lang="sa-Latn">ahorātrasya madhye tu jñeyā dvādaśa saṃkramāḥ | vṛṣakarkaṭakanyālimṛgamīnā niśākare ||73||</p>
  <p class="tr">Within a day and night, know there to be twelve transits. Taurus, Cancer, Virgo, Scorpio, Capricorn and Pisces belong to the moon. <span class="ref">— Verse 73</span></p>
</div>

The zodiac here is decoration. It is the standard even-and-odd division of the signs, even ones assigned to the left channel and odd to the right — two existing classifications laid on top of each other and presented as a discovery. There is no physiology in it.

But look past the signs at the number. Twelve transits across twenty-four hours is two hours per phase. That is not astrology. That is a measurement, stated as plainly as the text ever states anything, and it is buried under the part that is worthless.

This is not a minor discrepancy. On any given morning, the three figures disagree about which nostril should be open. A tool that picks one of them is making a choice the text does not make for it.

## Which of the three is closest

The twelve-transit figure, and it is not close.

Kahana-Zweig and colleagues logged airflow in both nostrils of 33 adults for about 24 hours each, and reported an average dominance period of 2.15 hours — roughly 129 minutes. Verses 73–74 say 120. The agreement is better than the text has any right to expect.

That a pre-modern tradition arrived at an approximately two-hourly figure for an autonomic rhythm that Western medicine did not describe until Kayser in 1895 is the substantive fact in this whole section. It survives the removal of the zodiac, the elements and the cosmology.

Two cautions, though, and they are large.

The first is that an average is not a rule. The same study found individual spells running from a quarter of an hour to more than ten. Any single number the tradition settled on would be wrong for most people most of the time.

The second is worse for the text. Two reviews of the rhinology literature — Hanif, Jawad and Eccles in *Clinical Otolaryngology* in 2000, and Pendolino and colleagues in *Rhinology Online* in 2018 — both report that only a minority of people show a genuinely regular, reciprocal cycle, with the two sides alternating on one steady rhythm. Older observational work put the figure as high as 80 per cent. The more careful quantitative studies brought it down to between 21 and 39 per cent. Gilbert and Rosenwasser put it bluntly in 1987: an ideal cycle is barely found.

So the text's two hours is a good estimate of a rhythm that a majority of people do not clearly have.

## The moment between

This is the part of the timing material I find most interesting, and almost nobody quotes it.

<div class="verse">
  <p class="dev" lang="sa">क्षणं वामे क्षणं दक्षे यदा वहति मारुतः। सुषुम्ना सा च विज्ञेया सर्वकार्यहरा स्मृता॥१२४॥</p>
  <p class="iast" lang="sa-Latn">kṣaṇaṃ vāme kṣaṇaṃ dakṣe yadā vahati mārutaḥ | suṣumnā sā ca vijñeyā sarvakāryaharā smṛtā ||124||</p>
  <p class="tr">When the breath flows a moment on the left and a moment on the right, know that to be <em>suṣumnā</em>, which is said to carry away all undertakings. <span class="ref">— Verse 124</span></p>
</div>

The definition is precise and it is physiologically correct. *Suṣumnā* is not a third channel with a flow of its own. It is the changeover — the window when the asymmetry passes through a minimum and neither side is stably dominant. That window is real and observable.

The claim attached to it is that action fails during it. As a statement about the world, that is false; the universe does not consult your nose. As a statement about your own performance during an autonomic transition, it is a specific and unexplored hypothesis, and the reasoning behind it is not silly. Transitions between states are periods of regulatory instability by definition, and systems approaching a transition are known to show increased variance and slower recovery from disturbance.

Nobody has tested whether people perform measurably worse on anything during the nasal changeover. It would not be hard to find out.

Each of these needs the same thing from the text, and the text supplies it: a number. "The breath changes every so often" cannot fail. "Twelve transits in a day and night" can.

## Why the ambiguity stays in the tool

The [Swar Yoga tool](/swar-yoga.html#svara) offers all three periods in a dropdown, each with its verse, and defaults to the shortest. I thought about hiding the choice and picking one. I decided against it, for a reason worth stating.

Pick one silently and the tool looks authoritative. It also becomes a claim I cannot defend, because the text does not make that choice and I have no evidence that would let me make it for the text. Show all three and the tool is honest about what it is: a reading of a document that disagrees with itself, offered so that your own record can eventually arbitrate between them.

The three figures give different answers on the same day. That is not a flaw to be smoothed over. It is the most useful thing the timing passages have to say, because it tells you exactly where to point a measurement.

The text is specific. Being specific is how it earns the right to be checked — and how it risks being found wrong. Most of what gets written about swara yoga carefully avoids both.
