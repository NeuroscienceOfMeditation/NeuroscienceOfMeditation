Feelings, things, sorrow take years to heal. As a human mind, we cannot get over things that fast — and maybe that is why we fake. We fake our smiles for a photo and our laughter for a video. We fake who we are, because we don't know who we are.

Yoga begins from the opposite direction. It does not ask us to perform a feeling. It asks us to observe.

## Guilt is not a reliable instrument

How can a person know whether they have betrayed their own conscience? Most of us answer psychologically: we speak of guilt, regret, shame, remorse. But guilt is an unreliable indicator. It can arise because we broke a social convention or disappointed someone, even when what we did was right. And a mind that has grown used to justifying itself can do real harm and feel nothing at all.

Patanjali does not ask us to trust whatever passes through the mind. His first definition is an instruction to examine the instrument before believing its readings:

<div class="verse">
  <p class="iast" lang="sa-Latn">yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ</p>
  <p class="tr">Yoga is the cessation of the modifications of the mind. <span class="ref">— Yoga Sūtra I.2</span></p>
</div>

A scientist does not trust a microscope with clouded lenses. The yogic tradition places real moral discernment not in feeling but in *buddhi*, the discriminating intellect, and in its capacity for *viveka* — telling the real from the unreal, the lasting from the passing. When *buddhi* is clear, truth is often recognised with remarkable simplicity. When it is clouded by desire, fear or self-image, the light goes dim.

## The displacement before the act

Patanjali's account of the *kleśas*, the afflictions, explains how the clouding happens. At the root is *avidyā*: not a lack of information, but mistaking the passing personality for the self. From that come attachment, aversion and fear. Every betrayal of conscience is preceded by a quiet shift of attention. We stop asking *what is true?* and start asking *what will protect my image?*

Psychology has its own name for part of this. Leon Festinger called it cognitive dissonance: the discomfort of acting against what we believe. People often relieve it not by changing what they do, but by changing the story they tell about it. The restless justifications of the mind are exactly the *vṛttis* that keep us from seeing clearly.

## A test you can run

So how would you know? Yoga's answer is not argument but experiment. Quiet the mind first — through ethical steadiness, the breath, withdrawing from constant stimulation, and meditation — and only then ask the question.

Don't ask *was I successful?* or *was I praised?* Ask this:

> As my mind becomes quieter, does this choice become clearer — or does it need constant argument to stay acceptable?

Truth does not need continual defence. It has a stability of its own. Falsehood needs maintenance: excuses repeated, memories edited, blame moved around. That is why silence can feel so uncomfortable. It removes the distractions that keep a contradiction hidden.

If, over many sittings, a choice keeps producing contraction, agitation and fresh justifications, don't condemn yourself; recognise that something is unresolved. If growing stillness brings growing simplicity, the instrument is beginning to work. The conclusion comes not from one emotional reaction but from repeated observation under steadier conditions, the way a scientist repeats an experiment to rule out chance.

## Clarity and comfort are different things

Here is the part that is easy to miss. People who have ignored their conscience for a long time can seem remarkably untroubled. The absence of discomfort is not evidence of innocence; the mind may simply have grown used to its own rationalisations. Every action leaves an impression, a *saṁskāra*, and repeated actions shape character — much as repeated behaviour strengthens pathways in the brain.

So the question is not whether guilt is present. It is whether clarity is present. Sometimes the first sign of waking up is not peace but the painful recognition of something long denied. That discomfort is not failure. It is the beginning of purification.

## Why no one can do this for you

A platform built around inner work can make a tempting promise: follow the method, and your suffering will be solved. We don't make that promise. A practice can help you feel calmer or sleep better, and that can matter enormously. But relief is not the same as understanding, and no teacher, text or technique can become the answer to a whole life.

A teacher can guide. A text can challenge. A study can constrain a claim. A practice can create the conditions for observation. None of these removes the need for you to observe, question, reflect and choose.

<div class="verse">
  <p class="iast" lang="sa-Latn">tadā draṣṭuḥ svarūpe'vasthānam</p>
  <p class="tr">Then the Seer abides in its own true nature. <span class="ref">— Yoga Sūtra I.3</span></p>
</div>

The world judges by appearances, the intellect by logic, the emotions by pleasure and pain. Yoga asks for something subtler: whether, in the stillness where every defence has dissolved and every excuse has gone quiet, the heart rests easily in what is true. If a choice shines more simply as the silence deepens, go ahead without fear. If it survives only on argument and noise, keep looking. Don't condemn yourself — but don't deceive yourself either.
