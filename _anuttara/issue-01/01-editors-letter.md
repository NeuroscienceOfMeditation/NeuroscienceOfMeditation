There is a moment most practitioners know. You have been told what a practice does — that it balances the brain, that the breath follows the moon, that ten minutes will change your life — and something in you wants to ask: *how do we know?*

Aṇūṭṭara is written for that moment.

I am a doctoral fellow in the science of yoga, which means working between two rooms that rarely talk to each other. In one, the texts: careful, strange, often astonishingly observant, sometimes plainly wrong. In the other, the laboratory: precise, sometimes narrow, and often unaware that the question it is asking was asked centuries ago. This magazine sits in the doorway between them.

## What you will find here

Each issue does three things.

**It reads the old texts closely**, with the Sanskrit alongside, and asks what each claim actually says before asking whether it is true. This issue begins with the *Śiva Svarodaya*, a text about which nostril the breath flows through, and what it noticed about the rhythm of the day.

**It reports what the research has found**, including the studies that disappoint. Every study is linked so you can read it yourself. When the evidence is small, we say so. When a popular claim has been tested and failed, we say that too.

**It gives you something to practise**, because none of this matters unless it changes what you do on an ordinary morning.

## What you will not find

You will not find promises. The practices in these pages are education, not treatment. Some of them help many people; none of them are a solution to a whole life, and a magazine that told you otherwise would be selling something.

*Anuttara* is often translated as "that which has nothing higher". I like it for a humbler reason: it is a name for the place questions lead to when you keep asking them honestly. That is where I hope these pages take you.

— Aditi
