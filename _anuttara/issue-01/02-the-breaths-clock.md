Breathe out gently through your nose and notice which side carries more air. For most people, most of the time, one nostril is more open than the other, and a few hours from now it will probably be the other way round.

The *Śiva Svarodaya* is built on that observation. It is a Sanskrit dialogue in which Śiva teaches Pārvatī to watch the breath's side, its timing and its changes, and to act according to what she finds. Read without its cosmology, it is one of the most detailed manuals of bodily self-observation written before the twentieth century. It is also, in places, plain astrology. This piece is about the difference.

## The rhythm is real

In 1895 the German rhinologist Richard Kayser documented what the text had assumed all along: in most healthy adults the two nasal passages are not equally open, and the asymmetry reverses at intervals. The mechanism is vascular. The lining of the nose contains spongy tissue that fills and drains under the control of the autonomic nervous system; when one side swells, the other opens. The alternation is driven from the brain, not the nose itself. It carries on through sleep, and it survives numbing the inside of the nose. <span class="grade g-e" title="Established">E</span>

So the first claim of the *Svarodaya* — that the breath has a side, and the side changes — is simply correct.

## What the text got right about the timing

The text also puts numbers on the rhythm.

<div class="verse">
  <p class="dev" lang="sa">सार्धद्विघटिके पञ्च क्रमेणैवोदयन्ति च।<br>क्रमादेकैकनाड्यां च तत्त्वानां पृथगुद्भवः॥७२॥</p>
  <p class="iast" lang="sa-Latn">sārdhadvighaṭike pañca krameṇaivodayanti ca |<br>kramād ekaikanāḍyāṃ ca tattvānāṃ pṛthag udbhavaḥ ||72||</p>
  <p class="tr">The five arise in sequence within two and a half ghaṭikās, each element arising distinctly in turn within each channel's flow. <span class="ref">— Verse 72</span></p>
</div>

A *ghaṭikā* is twenty-four minutes, so two and a half of them make an hour. Elsewhere the text gives two and a half *ghaṭikās* to each of the five phases instead, which makes the full period two and a half hours. The text contradicts itself, and different manuscripts settle the contradiction differently.

But the order of magnitude is right. The measured nasal cycle commonly runs somewhere between two and four hours, with enormous variation between people, and a sizeable minority of healthy people show no clear regular alternation at all. <span class="grade g-e" title="Established">E</span> Some researchers have linked it to the roughly ninety-to-hundred-and-twenty-minute rest–activity rhythm that runs through sleep and waking; the link is plausible but not established. <span class="grade g-s" title="Suggestive">S</span>

A pre-modern text describing a one-to-three-hour bodily rhythm is not making a lucky guess. It is reporting sustained observation. The contradiction in its numbers is informative too: when a tidy scheme is laid over an irregular natural rhythm, you get exactly this — several neat versions, none of which fits most people most of the time.

**What to take from this:** don't trust any fixed number, including the text's. Find your own rhythm by checking, and writing it down.

## The in-between moment

The text's most careful observation is about the changeover.

<div class="verse">
  <p class="dev" lang="sa">क्षणं वामे क्षणं दक्षे यदा वहति मारुतः।<br>सुषुम्ना सा च विज्ञेया सर्वकार्यहरा स्मृता॥१२४॥</p>
  <p class="iast" lang="sa-Latn">kṣaṇaṃ vāme kṣaṇaṃ dakṣe yadā vahati mārutaḥ |<br>suṣumnā sā ca vijñeyā sarvakāryaharā smṛtā ||124||</p>
  <p class="tr">When the breath flows a moment in the left and a moment in the right, know that as Suṣumnā; it is held to carry away all undertakings. <span class="ref">— Verse 124</span></p>
</div>

*A moment on the left, a moment on the right.* That is an exact description of the changeover in the nasal cycle, when neither side is clearly dominant and the balance flickers. <span class="grade g-e" title="Established">E</span>

The claim attached to it — that undertakings fail in this window — is false as a prediction about the world. As a claim about the person, it is more interesting. Everyone knows the state it seems to describe: the twenty minutes when you cannot get a grip on a task however hard you try. Whether that state is caused by the nasal changeover has never been tested. <span class="grade g-x" title="Speculative">X</span> It would be a simple experiment.

What the text recommends for this window is wiser than its prediction. Verses 129 and 130 say that in the irregular state one should turn the mind to what is ultimate and do only contemplative practice, *and nothing else*. Strip away the theology and the advice stands up well: the costly thing about an unproductive stretch is not the stretch itself but trying to force work through it, and switching back and forth between the task and giving up. Research on attention supports stopping cleanly, writing down the next step so it stops nagging, and doing something defined and different for a while — a walk is the best-supported choice. <span class="grade g-e" title="Established">E</span>

## Where the clock breaks

Then the text overreaches. It says the side that opens at sunrise depends on the phase of the moon, that certain weekdays belong to the left nostril and others to the right, that the zodiac passes through the channels in turn.

None of this has any physiological basis. Nasal dominance is not set by the day of the week or the lunar fortnight, and no mechanism by which it could be has ever been proposed. <span class="grade g-en" title="Established that it is false">E, negative</span> These rules are imported from astrology, and they are deduced from a symbol — the left channel is "the moon" — rather than observed in the body.

This is the pattern to watch for throughout the *Svarodaya*. Where it describes what you can check with your own breath, it is often remarkably accurate. Where it deduces the body from a metaphor, it goes wrong.

## Check it yourself

The text's own instrument is still the best one. Verse 153 tells the practitioner to breathe onto a mirror and look. Do that, or hold the back of your hand under your nose, a few times through a day. Note the time and the side. After a week you will know more about your own breath's clock than any fixed rule can tell you.
