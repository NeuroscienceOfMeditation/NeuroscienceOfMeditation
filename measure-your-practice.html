Measure your practice

What your heart can, and can't, tell you about meditation

What heart-rate variability is

Your heart does not beat like a metronome. The gap between two beats, called the RR interval, changes a little with every beat. Heart-rate variability (HRV) measures how much it changes.

The number most apps show is RMSSD: how much each beat-to-beat gap differs from the next. It is the main short-term measure of the vagus nerve's influence on the heart, the "rest and digest" side of the nervous system.

How sure is this? Established Shaffer & Ginsberg 2017, an overview of HRV measures (Frontiers in Public Health) ↗

The catch most apps skip

How you breathe changes RMSSD directly. Breathing slower and deeper makes the heart speed up and slow down more with each breath, which raises RMSSD, even if the vagus nerve's underlying tone has not changed. So a higher number during slow breathing is expected. It is not proof of lasting change.

How sure is this? Established Shaffer & Ginsberg 2017 ↗

What happens to the heart during meditation

The heart swings more, not less

In a 1999 study, 8 people practising Chi meditation and 4 practising Kundalini yoga wore heart monitors. During meditation their heart rate rose and fell with each slow breath, sometimes by 30 beats a minute within seconds, far more than at rest beforehand. Meditation was not a "quiet" state for the heart.

How sure is this? Suggestive12 people, no control group Peng et al. 1999, heart-rate oscillations during two meditation techniques (International Journal of Cardiology); data on PhysioNet ↗

Slow breathing calms

Breathing at around six breaths a minute is linked with higher HRV and with feeling calmer and more alert. Breathing between about 4.5 and 7.5 breaths a minute gathers the heart's swings into one strong rhythm of about one cycle every ten seconds.

How sure is this? Establishedconsistent, but most studies are small Zaccaro et al. 2018, systematic review of slow breathing (Frontiers in Human Neuroscience) ↗ Shaffer & Ginsberg 2017 ↗

Does regular practice raise your resting HRV?

That is the question most people really want answered, and it is not settled. Measuring it well means measuring at rest, at the same time each day, over weeks, and comparing averages, not single days. The self-experiment below is set up to do exactly that for one person: you.

Which device measures it well?

A chest strap reads the heart's electrical signal, like a hospital ECG. Rings, watches and phone cameras read the pulse with light (photoplethysmography), which works well when you're still and much less well when you move.

How the numbers are worked out

Beats shorter than 250 ms or longer than 2,500 ms are dropped, and so is any beat more than 25% away from both the last clean beat and the median of the five beats before it. A missed or extra beat changes the interval by 40% or more, while breathing changes it by much less from one beat to the next. RMSSD uses only pairs of clean beats next to each other. If more than 5% of beats are dropped, treat the result with care. Recordings shorter than about 5 minutes are fine for comparing yourself with yourself, but not with published norms.

Your morning log

One morning's HRV says very little: sleep, a late meal, alcohol or a cold can move it more than meditation does. What matters is the trend. Studies of athletes track a 7-day rolling average of the log of RMSSD, and how much it varies from day to day.

How sure is this? Suggestivedeveloped in athletes, a small case study Plews et al. 2012, HRV in elite triathletes (European Journal of Applied Physiology) ↗

A simple self-experiment

Does your practice change your resting HRV? You can't answer that from a feeling or a single reading, but six weeks of steady mornings can show you a pattern.

  1. Measure the same way every morning. After waking and using the bathroom, before coffee or your phone. Same position each day, sitting or lying. Breathe normally, not slowly, for 1–2 minutes with a chest strap or phone-camera app. Write down the RMSSD.
  2. Two weeks of baseline. Live as usual. This shows how much your number moves on its own.
  3. Four weeks with practice. Add your practice every day at another time of day, for example 10 minutes of breath meditation in the evening. Tick "Practised yesterday" in the log.
  4. Compare averages, not days. Look at the 7-day average in the last week of practice against the baseline weeks. A shift smaller than your normal day-to-day spread is probably noise.
  5. Note what else changed. Sleep, illness, alcohol, travel, hard exercise and stress all move HRV. If one of these changed too, it may explain the shift.

Want to see the slow-breathing effect today?

Record 3 minutes breathing normally, then 5 minutes at about six breaths a minute (in for 4–5 seconds, out for 5–6), then 3 minutes normally again. Load it into the tool above and you'll see the heart's swings grow and settle. Remember the catch: this shows breathing working on the heart in the moment, not a change in your baseline.

Please keep in mind

  • These devices and this page are not medical tools. A wearable cannot diagnose anything.
  • With an irregular heartbeat such as atrial fibrillation, or many extra beats, HRV numbers are not meaningful. If your device often warns about an irregular rhythm, see a doctor.
  • Higher is not always better, and numbers differ a lot between people. Compare yourself only with yourself, on the same device.
  • If tracking makes you anxious about your body, stop. The practice matters more than the number.

What we left out, for now

Closed-loop systems that change a sound, a light or a VR scene in real time from your brain or heart signals are an active research area. The studies are early and done in labs, so we don't suggest them as a home method yet. We'll add them when the evidence is stronger.
