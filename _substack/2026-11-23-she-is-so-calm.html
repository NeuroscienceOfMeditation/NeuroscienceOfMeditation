“She Is So Calm.” No — She Overthinks, and Sleeps All Day to Forget.

Someone said this about a woman I know. She is so calm.

She is not calm. She overthinks everything, runs the same conversation thirty times, and sleeps most of the weekend because being unconscious is the only reliable way she has found to stop thinking. From the outside, none of that is visible. What is visible is a quiet person who does not raise her voice.

I have been thinking about that gap, because we are heading into the part of the year where calm becomes a product. Every December the same thing happens. You need a new morning routine. You need to wake at five. You need a planner, a workout, a cold plunge, a gratitude practice, your finances in order and your whole life resolved before the first of January. By the time the year turns, most of us have assembled a list of twenty-seven things wrong with us.

So I want to go the other way. Instead of what to add, here is what I am not taking into the next year — and underneath it, the thing I keep coming back to, which is that stillness and shutdown look identical from the outside and are not the same event.

In brief

Calm has become an aesthetic. Low voice, beige room, unhurried mornings. That is a look, and a look can be worn by someone who is regulated and by someone who has simply run out of road.

Meditation is the practice I keep returning to, and I want to be careful about how I sell it, because the research is more modest than the wellness industry suggests. The honest version: it helps some people with some things, by about as much as other active treatments, and the brain-change story is shakier than you have been told.

And I am going to name the thing underneath the title. Sleeping all day to avoid your own mind is not rest. It can look like the most peaceful thing in the room.

Two kinds of still

This is the distinction I wish someone had drawn for me earlier.

Your autonomic nervous system regulates things you do not consciously control — heart rate, digestion, aspects of breathing. It has a sympathetic branch that mobilises you when something needs doing, and a parasympathetic branch associated with rest, digestion and recovery.

Both are necessary. We are not trying to switch the sympathetic branch off. Try crossing a busy road without it.

The problem is not activation. It is living in a way that leaves almost no opportunity to recover — every minute occupied, every notification answered, every free gap converted into something productive. Do that for long enough and you do not become calm. You become depleted, and depleted is quiet.

With that said, here is what I am leaving behind.

One: I am not giving my first waking minutes to my phone

You wake. Your eyes are barely open. Before you have worked out how you feel, your hand is already on the phone, and you have taken in twenty-five pieces of information before brushing your teeth. Someone's holiday. Someone's relationship. Someone's new business.

I am not going to tell you your phone is destroying your brain. That claim is bigger than the evidence. But your attention is genuinely captured before you choose — unexpected and emotionally charged things pull it automatically — and if you interrupt yourself constantly, sustained attention is harder to hold.

The question I keep asking is simpler than the neuroscience. Why does the first experience of my day have to be somebody else's life?

Try five minutes before the phone. Notice your breathing, the temperature of the room, whether you are tired or anxious or simply still waking. You do not have to achieve anything.

That is also a decent introduction to meditation, which is largely the practice of noticing where attention has gone and bringing it back. Your mind will wander to the to-do list. Bring it back. It is not about an empty mind. It is about a different relationship with your attention.

Two: I am not confusing being busy with being important

Ask someone how they are and they will tell you how busy they are. Exhaustion has become a personality.

There is a difference between a full life and a life with no room left to experience it. I want to stop treating being tired as evidence that I am doing something right.

You can be ambitious and still have an unhurried morning. You can have goals and take an afternoon off.

Three: I am not filling every quiet moment with something

Walking? Podcast. Cooking? Video. Eating? Scroll. Getting ready? Something on in the background.

I love a podcast. But I have started to wonder whether we have become so uncomfortable with silence that every available second has to be occupied.

Your brain does different kinds of work. There is focused attention on a task, and there are periods when the mind wanders, reflects and connects things. A set of regions often called the default mode network shows different activity patterns when you are not engaged in an externally demanding task, and it has been associated with internally directed thought and self-reflection. Brewer and colleagues reported in PNAS in 2011 that experienced meditators showed differences in default mode activity and connectivity compared with controls.

I want to be careful with that, because it gets overstated constantly. It does not mean meditation switches the network off, and it does not mean mind-wandering is automatically good for you. The relationship is messier than the version that gets repeated online.

What it does suggest is that the brain does not need to be fed external information every waking moment. When did you last walk without listening to anything? Eat without a screen? Sit by a window and look out of it?

Some moments are allowed to stay empty. That is not wasted time.

Four: I am not becoming a completely different person in January

Every December we become extremely ambitious on behalf of a woman who does not exist. Five a.m., six workouts, fifty books, perfect skin, perfect habits, perfect home.

Why are we treating our current selves as a problem to be fixed?

Here is the more useful frame. Neuroplasticity is the brain's capacity to change with experience and repeated practice — it is why learning an instrument or a language works. Meditation is a practice in the same sense. Each time you notice your attention has wandered and return it, you are practising attentional regulation. Ehmann and colleagues reviewed the long-term meditation literature in NeuroImage last year and found attention-related changes across studies, while being clear about how much the picture varies by practice and design.

What you repeatedly do matters. That is the defensible version, and it is enough.

You do not need to become a new person every January. You can become better acquainted with the one already here.

Five: I am not reacting to every thought, emotion and discomfort

Anxious, so we scroll. Lonely, so we open an app. Bored, so we shop. Overwhelmed, so we look for one more piece of information that might make it stop.

Emotional regulation is not suppressing feeling or pretending things are fine. It is being able to recognise what is happening and respond deliberately rather than automatically.

Say you are sitting quietly and remember something embarrassing from five years ago. Normally you would follow it for twenty minutes — what you said, what they thought, what you should have done. In practice you notice: I am remembering something embarrassing. Then you come back.

You do not have to fight the thought or argue it away. Thoughts are not instructions. Feelings are not always facts. An uncomfortable sensation does not necessarily mean something terrible is happening.

Six: I am not turning meditation into another performance metric

I know how this goes. We discover meditation, buy a cushion, download three apps, commit to forty-five minutes daily, miss a session on day three and conclude we are undisciplined.

Please do not. Meditation is not a productivity hack, and it is not a competition about sitting still. It is also not about stopping thought — you are a human being, your brain makes thoughts.

Five minutes. Sit somewhere comfortable. Let your shoulders drop. Notice the breath without forcing it. When your mind wanders, bring it back. If you have to do that fifty times, you have practised returning your attention fifty times. That is the practice.

Some days you will feel calm, some restless, some nothing at all. And some days sitting quietly brings up something uncomfortable — that is common, it is reported in the research, and you do not have to push through it. Open your eyes, move, do something grounding, or stop.

Seven: I am not mistaking constant stimulation for happiness

Open an app, something new. Refresh, something new. One video ends and the next is already running.

Dopamine is involved in motivation, learning and reward prediction. It helps you recognise and pursue things that might be rewarding. It is not the happiness chemical, and you are not permanently damaging your dopamine system by scrolling. That framing is everywhere and it is not accurate.

What is worth examining is how thoroughly we have trained ourselves to expect something new every few seconds, and then wondered why a book feels difficult and a slow afternoon feels like a problem.

I want to be able to enjoy a quiet Tuesday. That is the whole ambition.

Eight: I am not taking everyone else's emotions home

You spend an hour with someone who complains continuously and leave carrying it. You see an argument online and become emotionally invested in a disagreement with nothing to do with your life.

You can care about someone without being responsible for every feeling they have. You can listen without fixing. Being endlessly available is not the same as being a good person.

Nine: I am not waiting until I am wrecked to rest

We wait until we are exhausted, or ill, or until something gives out. Then we permit ourselves an afternoon off.

Rest has become something you earn by finishing. But the list does not end, and your body does not run on your productivity calendar.

Breathing is a useful place to start, because it is one of the few autonomic processes you can take over deliberately. Laborde and colleagues published a systematic review and meta-analysis in Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews in 2022 on voluntary slow breathing and heart rate variability, and Zaccaro and colleagues reviewed the psychophysiological correlates of slow breathing in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience in 2018. The general finding is that slow, comfortable breathing can shift cardiovascular and autonomic measures — with real variation between people, practices and protocols.

Sit comfortably. Unclench your jaw. Let the shoulders drop. Breathe in gently without forcing, and let the out-breath be a little longer and still comfortable. A few minutes. If you feel dizzy, go back to breathing normally.

Rest is not a reward for productivity. It is part of working at all.

Ten: I am not entering another year without spending time with myself

I know what everyone is doing, what is trending, what people are buying and watching. How much time am I spending understanding my own life?

Meditation gives you a chance to observe what is happening. Journaling gives you somewhere to put it. Sit for ten minutes, then write: what has been occupying my mind, what am I worrying about that I cannot control, what do I need more of, what am I doing only because everyone else is.

What the research actually supports

I am not going to recommend a practice on this site without telling you how strong the evidence is. For meditation, it is real and considerably more modest than the industry around it.

I have been through all of this at length in what meditation researchers say when they are talking to each other, so I will not rerun it here. The short version is that the grey-matter result everyone quotes — Hölzel and colleagues, 2011 — was a small early study, and the Kral trial above is the larger randomised work that has since complicated it. That is how this is supposed to go. The honest position sits between the two rather than with whichever one you prefer.

Van Dam and colleagues put the structural problem best in 2018: at that point only about 9 per cent of mindfulness-intervention research had reached the stage of being tested against an active control. Most of what you have read about meditation was measured against doing nothing in particular.

So: worth doing, on evidence supporting moderate benefit for some people, and not a nervous system rebuild. I would rather tell you that than oversell it and have you conclude you are broken when eight weeks does not fix your life.

The close

I am wary of writing something like this in a way that turns rest into another thing to be good at.

So let me put it plainly. The woman everyone describes as calm may be regulated, or she may be managing. You cannot tell from the outside, and neither can she always, which is why the useful question is not do I look calm but do I feel better afterwards.

If the answer is yes, that is rest and you should have more of it.

If the answer has been no for months — if you are sleeping through weekends and surfacing just as tired, if the quiet is avoidance rather than recovery — then no morning routine is the right tool, and that is worth saying to a doctor. Meditation can sit alongside treatment. It is not a replacement for it, and this site is not going to pretend otherwise.

For everyone else, the invitation is small. Five minutes today. Sit somewhere comfortable, put the phone out of reach, notice the breath, and when your mind wanders, come back.

You do not need to add ten things. You might need to put a few down.
