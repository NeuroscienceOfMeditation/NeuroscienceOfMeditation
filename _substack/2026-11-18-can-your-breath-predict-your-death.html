Can Your Breath Tell You When You Are Going to Die?

Most summaries of the Śiva Svarodaya stop around the middle. They give you the nostrils, the elements, the lunar day, perhaps the practices. What they leave out is that roughly the last quarter of the text is a manual for predicting death.

It is not a vague memento mori. It is specific. It tells you how many months a person has, which sign gives which interval, and what to conclude if the breath has stopped changing sides. I have been working through this section for the Respiratory Codex, and I kept putting the write-up off, because it is the part of the treatise where the stakes are highest and the method is worst.

Then I noticed something that made it worth publishing. The timetable is invented. But the signs the tradition collected are, several of them, excellent — in a couple of cases, they are findings I would want someone to go to a hospital about today. The failure is very precisely located, and locating it is more interesting than dismissing the whole thing.

What the text actually claims

I will quote the claims plainly, because specificity is what makes them testable.

On the breath itself, verses 331 to 334. If the breath flows on one side for a full day and night, death follows at the completion of three years. Two days and nights in the right channel, two years. Three nights in one nostril, one year. If the left flows continuously at night and the right by day without interruption, six months.

Then a descending series. No sensation in the skin of the chest: five months. No flash of light when the eyes are pressed: four months. The right channel flowing continuously with the left not seen at all: a fortnight. Urine, faeces and wind passing together: ten days.

There is also a bedside procedure. A visitor asks about a sick person, and the answer is read from which side of the patient the visitor is standing on relative to the patient's open nostril. Verse 323 is the one that decided how I had to write this piece: if the breath is in the left channel while the enquirer stands on the right, the patient will die yadi vaidya-śatair vṛtaḥ — though surrounded by a hundred physicians.

And there is a shadow practice: stand with the sun behind you, fix on your own shadow at the neck while reciting a formula one hundred and eight times, then look at the sky. A form appears. Black means six months.

The intervals are a scheme, not a measurement

Look at the numbers as a set rather than one at a time: three years, two years, one year, six months, five months, four months, a fortnight, ten days.

That is a descending series of round figures, and the signs are ordered by how alarming they look. The more dramatic the sign, the shorter the interval. This is intuitively reasonable and formally invalid, because the relationship between how dramatic a sign appears and how much prognostic weight it carries is not close at all.

I want to be fair to the tradition here. Assigning shorter intervals to graver-looking signs is not stupid; it is what anyone would do before survival analysis existed. It is simply not evidence, and the smoothness of the series is the tell.

The breath claim fails on its own terms

Set the timetable aside and take only the physiological claim: that a breath which stops alternating is a sign of approaching death.

Persistent one-sided nasal obstruction is common and almost always boring. A deviated septum produces fixed asymmetry that masks or abolishes the cycle. So do nasal polyps, chronic rhinosinusitis, allergic rhinitis, turbinate hypertrophy and old trauma. None of these predicts death at any interval. Several of them are treatable, and Hanif, Jawad and Eccles make the point in Clinical Otolaryngology that the cycle is routinely disturbed by exactly this sort of ordinary nasal disease.

There is a second problem, and it is worse for the text. A substantial share of healthy people do not show classical alternation at all. I went through this in detail in Is the Nasal Cycle Even a Cycle? — regular, reciprocal, clock-like switching is the textbook picture and the minority finding. Apply verse 333 to a normal population and you hand a terminal prognosis to a large fraction of perfectly well people.

So: if your nostrils do not seem to alternate, the overwhelmingly likely explanation is anatomical or inflammatory. That is a reason to see an ENT surgeon, where the differential includes things worth catching early. It is not a reason to count months.

Why a false prognosis is not merely useless

I would normally leave it there. The phrase at verse 323 is why I cannot.

A confident prediction of death, delivered with authority, is not inert. It acts on three things at once.

It acts on the patient. Negative expectation produces measurable symptom worsening and physiological change — Colloca and Barsky's review in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2020 sets out the mechanisms, and they are not mysterious. The size of this should not be overstated; expectation does not determine the course of serious organic disease. But it is not zero, and it runs in the direction the pronouncement specifies.

It acts on the family, who make different decisions once they have been told the outcome is settled. Care is sought later, or not sought.

And it acts on the effort anyone expends, including the patient's own. "Though surrounded by a hundred physicians" is an instruction to stop trying.

That combination — an invalid method, delivered confidently, with effects that run the same way as the prediction — is what makes this section worse than merely wrong. A useless test wastes time. A useless test that people act on does something else.

The part I did not expect

Here is the turn. Strip out every interval and read the section as a list of things worth noticing, and it becomes the strongest clinical material in the treatise.

Sudden derangement of mind. Verse 337 names akasmāt citta-vikṛti — sudden alteration of the mind — as the foremost sign, before anything else on the list. This is correct, and I think it is the single best clinical observation in the book. Acute change in mental state is the cardinal feature of delirium, and delirium is not an incidental accompaniment of being ill. Witlox and colleagues pooled the follow-up studies in JAMA in 2010: across seven studies with 714 delirium patients and 2,243 controls, mortality was 38.0 per cent against 27.5 per cent over an average 22.7 months, an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.95. Institutionalisation and subsequent dementia were raised too. And the emphasis on akasmāt, suddenness, is exactly the right emphasis — abrupt onset and a fluctuating course are what separate delirium from dementia.

Urine, faeces and wind passing together. The text makes this ten days. In a clinic, new combined bladder and bowel incontinence — particularly with back pain, leg weakness or numbness in the saddle area — is a possible cauda equina syndrome and is assessed the same day. Kumar and colleagues pooled 22 studies and 852 cases in the European Spine Journal in 2022: persistent bladder dysfunction in 24.6 per cent of those decompressed within 48 hours of symptom onset, against 50.3 per cent where mean time to decompression was longer. The authors are careful that this is not conclusive and that a randomised trial is needed. It is still the most urgent item in the chapter, and the text has it on the list.

Loss of sensation over the chest. A well-demarcated sensory level on the trunk is a classic sign of a spinal cord lesion. It is also what you would elicit by precisely the method implied — testing whether sensation is there.

No flash when the eye is pressed. Press gently on a closed eye and you see a phosphene, because mechanical deformation depolarises retinal cells in the absence of light. The perception needs an intact retina, an intact optic nerve and a working visual cortex. Its absence in one eye is a reason for an ophthalmologist, not a countdown. (And press gently, or not at all with glaucoma, retinal disease, recent eye surgery or high myopia.)

A halo around a lamp or the moon. Eleven months, says verse 340. New haloes around lights with eye pain, redness, headache or nausea can be acute angle-closure glaucoma, which is an emergency and can take vision permanently within hours.

Sudden wasting of the body. Unintentional weight loss is a real warning sign. Nicholson and colleagues reviewed 25 studies in the British Journal of General Practice in 2018 and found that in patients aged 60 and over presenting with unexpected weight loss, the probability of an underlying cancer exceeded the 3 per cent threshold that triggers urgent investigation in UK primary care.

Six real red flags and one piece of nonsense, all in the same chapter, graded by the same method. That is what an observational tradition without a way to test itself produces: it cannot tell its best finding from its worst.

Where the instinct was right, and how far

The underlying intuition — that a dying body shows it, and that a trained observer can read it — is not wrong. It has been measured.

David Hui and colleagues recorded bedside signs every twelve hours in 357 patients with advanced cancer on two palliative care units, in Texas and in Brazil, and published it in The Oncologist in 2014. Five signs turned out to be highly specific for death within three days.

Three honest limits on that. These signs are specific but not sensitive, so their absence tells you very little. The cohort was advanced cancer on specialist palliative units, not the general population. And the window is three days — not three years.

Which is the exact shape of the error in the Svarodaya. The tradition was right that the body signals, right about several of the signals, and wrong by three orders of magnitude about the horizon.

The one idea worth keeping

There is a real principle under verse 331, and it is worth stating properly because the text almost has it.

Variability in a physiological rhythm is generally a marker of health, and its loss is generally a marker of derangement. That is true, and it is best evidenced in the heart rather than the nose. Kleiger and colleagues followed 808 patients after myocardial infarction and reported in the American Journal of Cardiology in 1987 that those whose 24-hour heart rate variability fell below an SDNN of 50 milliseconds had substantially higher mortality. Loss of variability has since become one of the more durable risk markers in cardiology.

Note carefully what that is and is not. It is a population-level risk marker, derived from a measured signal, validated against actual deaths. It is not a clock, it does not tell an individual how long they have, and nothing licenses transferring it to a rhythm where no such work has been done. The Svarodaya reached for the right idea with the wrong organ and no data, which is roughly how it does everything.

If you have just gone and checked your nostrils

Two things.

If one side is persistently blocked, that is an ENT question, and a worth-asking one. Septal deviation, polyps and chronic rhinosinusitis are common, diagnosable and treatable.

If you find yourself checking repeatedly, that is the thing to act on. Repeated bodily checking in search of a feared sign is the mechanism that maintains health anxiety rather than a way of resolving it, and the checking reliably increases the fear rather than settling it. If reading this has started that, the answer is to stop checking — and if it does not stop, health anxiety is common and treatable, and worth raising with a doctor.

The close

There is a passage near the end of the treatise that I keep thinking about. At verse 265 the text admits that the breath cannot always be read — through circumstance, or through inattention. That is an honest admission and an accurate one. Verse 266 then gives the remedy: hold still, let a flower fall from your hand, and see which side it falls on. The text calls this decisive.

A real measurement and a randomiser, offered as equivalents, with no difference in status asserted between them. Nobody outside the tradition could level a sharper criticism than the text levels at itself there.

I do not think that makes the Śiva Svarodaya worthless. I think it makes it a record of people watching bodies very carefully for a very long time, with no method for separating what they had found from what they had assumed. They noticed that a sudden change of mind is the gravest thing you can see in a sick person. They also noticed which side a flower falls on. Both went into the same book with the same authority.

The only thing I would ask of this chapter is to read it as a list of signs and throw the timetable away. Done that way, it says: sudden confusion, loss of sensation, loss of continence, a change in vision, a body wasting without reason. Those are not months on a calendar. They are reasons to see a doctor this week, and in two cases today.
