Three weeks ago I published [a piece about the eightfold scheme](/seven-things-to-measure.html) in the *Śiva Svarodaya*, and in it I made a claim about the mirror test of verse 153. I wrote that as far as I could find, nobody had validated mirror condensation against rhinomanometry or acoustic rhinometry, and that it was a plausible measurement rather than a validated one.

The second half of that sentence has held up. The first half has not. People have tested it, repeatedly, since the nineteenth century. I had simply been searching for it under the wrong name.

What they found is more interesting than the gap I thought I was reporting, and it undermines something I recommended, so it seems worth a post of its own rather than a correction note at the bottom of the old one.

## In brief

The mirror has a name. Zwaardemaker described it, Glatzel popularised it, and rhinology has used the **Glatzel mirror** as a bedside patency test for over a century: breathe onto a cold metal plate, trace the two patches of condensation, compare them.

It is reproducible. Measured minute by minute, hour by hour, day by day and week by week, the total condensation area varies by about eleven per cent.

It does not match what you feel. In twenty-five healthy adults, the correlation between condensation area and a visual analogue scale of how well they thought they were breathing was 0.04.

It does not match a flow meter either. In sixty-four children, the correlation between condensation area and peak nasal inspiratory flow was not significant in any group tested.

So I was wrong about the literature, and the literature is not kind to the use I was putting the instrument to.

## What I actually recommended, and why it matters

Here is the sentence I would now take back. In the Swar Yoga tool and in that post, I described the mirror as the calibration step for interoceptive training: judge which nostril is open, *then* check with the mirror, and let the repeated pairing of judgement with feedback train the discrimination.

That recommendation has a hidden premise. It assumes the mirror is the referee — that when your sense and the patch disagree, the patch is right. If the mirror does not track airflow any better than your nose does, then what I built was not a calibration loop. It was two uncertain readings being compared with each other, with one of them arbitrarily given the authority.

That is the kind of mistake worth publishing, because it is not an error about a fact in a thousand-year-old text. It is an error in something I told people to do this month.

## The instrument, and its real history

The procedure described at verse 153 is this: look into a mirror, cast the breath upon it, and read the difference. The text then spoils it by asking you to read a square, a crescent, a triangle or a circle off the patch and infer an element — which I have argued elsewhere is where the information is lost.

Strip the shapes away and the remaining procedure is a recognised clinical test. Brescovici and Roithmann, writing in the *Brazilian Journal of Otorhinolaryngology* in 2008, describe it as "an old and simple technique for objectively and momentarily verifying the nasal patency", consisting of observing the condensation of exhaled air on a cold metal surface. They credit Zwaardemaker with the first description and Glatzel with its popularisation.

Their modified version is worth stating in full, because it is the version with numbers attached. The subject sits upright. A metal plate is held horizontally under the nostrils with the zero mark beneath the columella. The subject breathes slowly through both nostrils, eyes and mouth closed, with no effort. The first condensation is discarded and the second is traced. Each patch is measured along a greater and a lesser axis against a millimetre scale, and the area is calculated as an ellipse.

Which is to say: a tantric manual and a Brazilian ENT department arrived at the same instrument. The difference is that one of them put a ruler on it.

## How reliable is it?

This is where the paper is genuinely useful, because reproducibility is the question I should have asked first.

Brescovici and Roithmann measured twenty-five healthy adults — fourteen men, eleven women, aged twenty-two to forty-seven — on a punishing schedule. Five readings a minute apart. Then every half hour for four hours. Then daily. Then weekly for five weeks.

They report variability as coefficients of variation rather than as an agreement statistic. Minute to minute, the median coefficient of variation was eleven per cent for total condensation area and about fourteen per cent for each side separately. Across hours, days and weeks it stayed in the same band: eleven, eleven and twelve per cent for the total.

A caveat I should state rather than bury. A coefficient of variation is not the same as agreement with a reference, and it does not tell you the instrument is measuring the right thing. A broken clock has excellent reproducibility. What it tells you is that the mirror is not merely noise, which is more than I assumed.

## The part that undoes my recommendation

Brescovici and Roithmann also asked their subjects, each time, to rate how well they felt they were breathing on a visual analogue scale, and compared that with the measured area.

The correlation for total area was **0.04**, with a p-value of 0.38. Left side, 0.08. Right side, 0.05. Nothing. Only about a third of subjects showed any significant positive correlation at all, and sixteen per cent showed a negative one — their nose felt clearer when the mirror said it was not.

Their conclusion is blunt: "We did not observe any significant correlation between the subjective perception of breathing and the condensation area."

Now, that finding on its own does not convict the mirror. It could equally convict the nose. We already know the felt sense of nasal patency is poor: Chin and colleagues measured 145 patients by anterior rhinomanometry in 2014 and found that self-assessment of the more obstructed side had a sensitivity of 86.9 per cent but a specificity of only 41.1 per cent. People notice real obstruction tolerably well and invent it freely when the sides are in fact symmetrical. Mozzanica and colleagues, measuring 233 patients with septal deviation in 2016, found a visual analogue scale correlated with rhinomanometric resistance at 0.372 — significant, and not strong. A systematic review by André and colleagues in 2009 concluded, as Mozzanica's group summarise it, that the correlation between subjective and objective assessment was uncertain.

So two weak measures disagreeing tells you little about which is weaker. For that you need to put the mirror against an instrument.

## Against a flow meter

Someone did. Melo, Santos, Perilo, Becker and Motta, in *CoDAS* in 2013, measured sixty-four children aged four to twelve — thirty-two mouth breathers and thirty-two nasal breathers — with both the Glatzel mirror and a peak nasal inspiratory flow meter, and correlated the two.

Not one correlation reached significance. Across all mouth breathers, Spearman's coefficient was −0.07. In the nasal-breathing controls it was 0.33, with a p-value of 0.07. The subgroups were worse and smaller. The authors' summary: "Low correlation was found between these two instruments."

In the discussion they go further than I had, and say plainly that the literature lacks validation studies for the mirror, that there are no reference values or standardisation data, and that the most specific tests currently available are rhinomanometry and acoustic rhinometry.

Three honest limits before anyone over-reads that triangle. The Melo study is in children, and children are harder to measure than adults on both instruments. The numbers in each subgroup are small — one reported coefficient rests on so few children that it came out at exactly −1.00, which is a sample-size artefact, not a finding. And a peak *inspiratory* flow meter and a patch of *expiratory* condensation are not measuring the same stroke of the breath, so some disagreement is expected on physical grounds alone.

None of that rescues my claim. It just means the right verdict is "still unvalidated, and the one instrumented comparison that exists did not go well", rather than "refuted".

## What the mirror can still do

One finding in the Melo paper points the other way, and I do not want to bury it because it is inconvenient.

The mirror did separate the groups. Mouth breathers and nasal breathers had different condensation areas, and the authors say the test was reliable in identifying participants with and without nasal obstruction, while being unable to distinguish the causes.

That is a meaningful distinction. An instrument can be useless for ranking two individuals and still useful for telling apart two populations that differ a lot. It means the patch is not measuring nothing. It is carrying a coarse signal about gross obstruction, and losing the fine differences — which is exactly what an eleven per cent coefficient of variation would predict.

Apply that to the swara yoga use case and the news is bad, because the swara yoga use case is the fine one. The tradition is not asking whether you have a deviated septum. It is asking which of your two nostrils is *more* open, right now, in a healthy nose, where the difference may be small and will reverse itself within a few hours anyway — and it then wants to hang the rest of the morning on the answer.

## So what do I do with my own tool

Three changes, and I would rather state them than quietly ship them.

**The mirror stops being the referee.** I will keep it in the tool, because an external trace is still better than nothing and because it costs nothing to take. But I will stop describing it as the check that settles the question, and the interface line that implies your judgement was right or wrong against it has to go.

**The honest framing is agreement, not accuracy.** What you can actually learn by pairing your judgement with a patch is whether the two agree, and whether that agreement changes over weeks of practice. That is a real and interesting question. It is simply not the same as learning whether you were correct, and I presented it as though it were.

**If you want to know, buy the meter.** A peak nasal inspiratory flow meter costs very little and is validated, which is more than either the mirror or your nose can say. Hoang and Ho published normal values from three hundred healthy adults last year — 158.8 ± 41.0 litres per minute in men, 113.4 ± 31.9 in women — and even that has a standard deviation of about a quarter of the mean, which should calibrate everyone's expectations about how precise this whole domain is.

## The close

I am wary of writing a post like this in a way that makes the correction sound like a virtue. It is not. I made a claim about a literature on the strength of a search that did not turn it up, and the reason it did not turn up is that I searched for the procedure rather than the instrument's name. That is an ordinary research failure and it would have been caught by anyone in rhinology in about four seconds.

What I will say in my own defence is that the thing I got wrong made the tradition look *better* than the evidence supports, not worse. I had the mirror down as an untested but promising instrument. It turns out to be a tested and underwhelming one. The correction moves in the direction of less confidence, which is at least the right direction for this site.

And the underlying picture has not changed since [the piece on whether the cycle is even a cycle](/is-the-nasal-cycle-even-a-cycle.html). The nasal cycle is real — Kahana-Zweig and colleagues established that carefully in 2016 — but it is irregular, it is only clearly periodic in a minority of people, and every method we have for reading it at the bedside is noisier than the certainty with which it gets taught.

Verse 153 reached for an external instrument when it could have carried on trusting the feeling. That instinct was right, and it is still the best thing in the chapter. What nobody told the author, and what I failed to tell you three weeks ago, is that reaching for an instrument is only half the job. The other half is finding out whether the instrument works.
