Nadi Shodhana without the mythology

Alternate nostril breathing does something measurable. It also gets credited with a great deal it has never been shown to do. Here is the line between the two.

Nadi Shodhana, or alternate nostril breathing, is one of the practices most frequently described as both ancient wisdom and modern medicine. Those two descriptions should not automatically be treated as the same claim.

What the practice actually involves

The basic pattern is simple: breathing is alternated through the left and right nostrils in a deliberate sequence. Different traditions describe different counts, pauses and techniques, so the name alone does not tell us exactly what a participant has practiced.

What can be measured?

Research can examine outcomes such as respiratory rate, heart-rate variability, blood pressure, subjective stress, attention, and other physiological or behavioural measures. Those are measurable outcomes. Claims about energy channels, purification, or a specific metaphysical effect require a different kind of evidence.

Where the evidence becomes uncertain

A measurable change after a breathing exercise does not by itself establish every traditional explanation attached to that exercise. Study design, comparison conditions, sample size and the exact breathing protocol all matter when interpreting a finding.

Tradition and evidence can coexist

The point is not to dismiss traditional practice. It is to separate the experience of the practice from the claims made about why it works. That distinction lets us investigate the practice seriously without asking science to validate something it was never designed to test.
