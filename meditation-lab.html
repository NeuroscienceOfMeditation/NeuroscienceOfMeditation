The hidden rhythm

Breaking a heart-rate recording into its rhythms shows which speeds of rise-and-fall are strongest. During meditation most of these people developed one dominant rhythm, a slow swing of roughly 2.5 to 6.5 cycles a minute, one every 9 to 23 seconds.

The study estimated breathing from the ECG itself and found that these swings closely tracked it: they were the heart following very slow breaths. This is respiratory sinus arrhythmia, the heart speeding up as you breathe in and slowing as you breathe out, made unusually large.

Why a wearable might miss it

RMSSD, the number most HRV apps show, measures how much the heart changes from one beat to the very next. Slow, smooth swings change it only a little per beat. So while the swings grew in 10 of these 12 people, RMSSD actually fell in 7 of them. One number never tells the whole story.

What this shows, and what it doesn't

It shows

In 10 of these 12 people, the heart swung harder during meditation than before. On average the swings were about three times those of the 34 non-meditators, and 6 of the 12 swung harder than any non-meditator, fit athletes included. Meditation here was not a quiet, flat state for the heart. It was rhythmic and lively.

Look closer

The Kundalini session was active work. Breathing exercises and chanting pushed average heart rate from about 61 to 92. The Chi meditators' "before" was recorded during an ordinary day, not quiet rest. And two Chi meditators showed no rise at all.

It doesn't show

Whether this is good for you, whether it lasts after the session, or whether meditation itself, rather than slow breathing, causes it. Twelve people, two traditions, one session each. The paper asks these questions; it does not answer them.

About the data

These are the original heart-rate recordings from the study, shared openly on PhysioNet under the Open Data Commons Attribution licence. Each person's instantaneous heart rate was measured from a Holter ECG, beat by beat.

We removed values outside 30–200 beats a minute and the rare beat that jumped more than 45% from its neighbours (under 1% in every recording), then resampled at 2 Hz. Nothing else was changed. The analysis code is public.
