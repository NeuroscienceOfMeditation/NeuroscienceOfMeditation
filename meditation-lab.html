The Meditation Lab

Real recordings from meditators. Look for yourself.

Most articles tell you what a study found. Here you can open the actual data behind two: the beat-by-beat heart rate of 12 meditators, and the brain waves of 24 meditators in the moments before they noticed their mind had wandered.

Heart · Peng et al. 1999

What one meditator's heart did

Choose a person. Each was recorded before meditating and then during it. Chi meditators (C) practised a guided visualisation and breathed as they liked; most had been practising for only 1–3 months. Kundalini Yoga meditators (Y) were advanced, and their session was a sequence of breathing and chanting exercises.

The hidden rhythm

Breaking a heart-rate recording into its rhythms shows which speeds of rise-and-fall are strongest. During meditation most of these people developed one dominant rhythm, a slow swing of roughly 2.5 to 6.5 cycles a minute, one every 9 to 23 seconds.

The study estimated breathing from the ECG itself and found that these swings closely tracked it: they were the heart following very slow breaths. This is respiratory sinus arrhythmia, the heart speeding up as you breathe in and slowing as you breathe out, made unusually large.

How sure is this? Suggestivebreathing was estimated from the ECG, not measured directly Peng et al. 1999, International Journal of Cardiology ↗

Why a wearable might miss it

RMSSD, the number most HRV apps show, measures how much the heart changes from one beat to the very next. Slow, smooth swings change it only a little per beat. So while the swings grew in 10 of these 12 people, RMSSD actually fell in 7 of them. One number never tells the whole story.

What this shows, and what it doesn't

It shows

In 10 of these 12 people, the heart swung harder during meditation than before. On average the swings were about three times those of the 34 non-meditators, and 6 of the 12 swung harder than any non-meditator, fit athletes included. Meditation here was not a quiet, flat state for the heart. It was rhythmic and lively.

Look closer

The Kundalini session was active work. Breathing exercises and chanting pushed average heart rate from about 61 to 92. The Chi meditators' "before" was recorded during an ordinary day, not quiet rest. And two Chi meditators showed no rise at all.

It doesn't show

Whether this is good for you, whether it lasts after the session, or whether meditation itself, rather than slow breathing, causes it. Twelve people, two traditions, one session each. The paper asks these questions; it does not answer them.

Brain · Brandmeyer & Delorme 2018

Meditating, or mind wandering? The brain, ten seconds before the bell

24 practitioners of the Himalayan Yoga tradition repeated a mantra in silence for 45 to 90 minutes, wearing a 64-electrode EEG cap. Every two minutes or so a voice interrupted them and asked three questions: how deep was your meditation, how much was your mind wandering, and how drowsy are you, each from 0 to 3. Twelve were experts, meditating at least two hours a day for a year or more; twelve practised less.

Experts wandered less

Each dot is one person. Leaving out check-ins where the two ratings were equal, experts rated their mind as wandering more than meditating about 25% of the time; the less experienced, about 57%. Our count from the experiment's own logs matches the paper's (24.6% and 57.3%).

Ten seconds of brain waves

The raw EEG from six electrodes, front to back, just before one check-in rated "meditating" and one rated "mind wandering".

Each row is one electrode: Fz at the front of the head, C3 and C4 over the left and right body-sensing (somatosensory) cortex, Cz on top, Pz behind it, Oz at the back. Brain waves are tiny, a few millionths of a volt, and raw recordings always look busy; the patterns show up when many windows are averaged, below.

Which brain waves changed

Brain waves are named by speed. Theta (4–7 cycles a second) over the front of the head is linked with focused, effortful attention. Alpha (9–11 cycles a second) is often read as the brain turning down incoming signals, here over the body-sensing cortex. Compare the two states below.

Agrees with the study

In experts, theta over the front of the head was higher when meditating than when the mind had wandered: about 14% on average, in 7 of 9 people. This is the paper's main finding, and our simpler re-analysis finds it too.

Doesn't fully agree

The paper also found more alpha over the body-sensing cortex in experts. We found only a small rise, about 6%, too small to be sure of. And in the less experienced group we found less alpha when meditating, 25% lower at the front of the head in all 9 people, where the paper found no difference.

Keep in mind

Mind-wandering moments were also the drowsy ones: about 0.9 out of 3 against 0.4–0.5 when meditating. Sleepiness changes alpha and theta too. After cleaning, 9 experts and 9 others had enough data. And the paper found no significant difference between the groups, so these data can't show that experts' brains work differently.

About the data

Everything on this page comes from data the researchers shared openly. We changed nothing except the cleaning described here.

Heart

The original heart-rate recordings from Peng et al., shared on PhysioNet under the Open Data Commons Attribution licence, measured beat by beat from a Holter ECG. We removed values outside 30–200 beats a minute and the rare beat that jumped more than 45% from its neighbours (under 1% in every recording), then resampled at 2 Hz.

Brain

The EEG recordings from Brandmeyer and Delorme, shared on OpenNeuro under CC0 (public domain), with the experiment's own log of every button press. Our analysis is simpler than the paper's: we filtered 1–40 Hz, repaired noisy electrodes, removed eye-movement signals with ICA, and dropped any 10-second window with a swing over 200 microvolts. The authors cleaned their data by hand and used cluster statistics, so our numbers can differ from theirs.
