Of all the ways to meditate, mantra is the one most people have already done without calling it that. A word said over and over, aloud or silently, until the saying is all there is. It is the oldest instruction in the Indian traditions and the one modern labs have found easiest to study, because a repeated word is something you can time, count and compare.

So what does it do? There are three kinds of answer in the literature, and they deserve different amounts of trust. I will take them in order of how sure we can be.

## What it does to how you feel

Start with the outcome most people care about. In 2022 Álvarez-Pérez and colleagues pooled 51 studies of mantra-based meditation, 27 of them on Transcendental Meditation and 24 on other mantra practices. Compared with control groups, mantra practice reduced anxiety (25 studies, 1,825 people), depression (22 studies) and stress (14 studies), with effects in the small-to-moderate range.

That is a real finding, and I would grade it established. But read the caveats the authors themselves give. No study in the review was rated at low risk of bias. The effects shrank when mantra was compared with an active alternative rather than with nothing, and against relaxation training the difference in anxiety was not significant. So: mantra meditation helps, about as much as other ways of sitting quietly with a structured task. There is no evidence here that it helps *more*.

## What it does to the brain's activity

The clearest brain finding comes from a study that did not use a traditional mantra at all.

Berkovich-Ohana and colleagues put 23 people with no meditation experience into a scanner and asked them to repeat silently, for 21 seconds at a time, the Hebrew word for "one". Compared with resting, the repetition did not light anything up. It switched things off: activity dropped across wide areas of the cortex, centred on the default mode network, the set of regions most active when the mind drifts into memories, plans and the story of oneself. A separate group reported fewer thoughts during repetition. Breathing did not differ between conditions, so it was not simply slower breathing doing the work.

The authors' own question mark in the title — the "Mantra" effect? — is fair. It was one word, short blocks, beginners, and a single study. I grade it suggestive. But it points at a plausible mechanism: a simple repeated task occupies just enough of the machinery that would otherwise wander.

A smaller pilot in 2011 found something similar for "OM". Twelve people chanted aloud in the scanner, and compared with a breath-matched "ssss" sound, OM came with reduced activity in a set of limbic regions, including the amygdala and the anterior cingulate. Twelve people, no replication, and a general relaxation effect cannot be ruled out. Suggestive at best.

When Fox and colleagues pooled 78 brain-imaging studies in 2016, mantra recitation stood out as its own pattern: more activity in the areas that plan and produce speech (premotor cortex, the supplementary motor area, the putamen), which is what you would expect from saying a word over and over. But the authors flagged the mantra results as resting heavily on two tiny studies, of five and four people, and called the largest effect sizes "highly questionable". Drop those two and part of the pattern disappears.

So the fMRI picture, graded honestly: repeating a word recruits speech areas and seems to quiet the mind-wandering network. Suggestive, consistent, and thin.

## What it does to brain waves

EEG measures the brain's electrical rhythms through the scalp. It is cheaper and faster than a scanner, and it can follow a meditator minute by minute, which is why most of the newer mantra research uses it.

The background first. Across 56 EEG studies of mindfulness, the most common finding was more alpha and theta — the slower rhythms linked with relaxed but alert attention — though not in every study, and the methods differed too much to pool. Mantra studies sit inside that general picture rather than overturning it.

The best-designed mantra study I know of is one you can explore yourself in the [Meditation Lab](/meditation-lab.html#brain). Brandmeyer and Delorme recorded 24 practitioners of the Himalayan Yoga tradition, whose practice ends in silent mantra concentration, for 45 to 90 minutes each with a 64-electrode cap. A voice interrupted them at random intervals and asked how deep their meditation was and how much their mind was wandering. Experienced meditators reported less wandering. And in the experienced group only, the moments rated as meditating showed more frontal-midline theta and more alpha over the body-sensing cortex than the moments rated as wandering. Twenty-four people, one tradition, no non-meditator comparison. Suggestive — but it asks the right question, because it compares a person with themselves at the moment it matters.

Three recent studies from Indian groups take a different route: they use machine learning to tell meditative states apart.

- Gupta, Beuria and Behera (2024) recorded 90 young adults with no meditation practice, in three groups of 30: one reflecting on their own past, one chanting the Hare Krishna mahāmantra, one sitting silently with eyes closed. With a four-sensor consumer headband, features from a branch of mathematics called topological data analysis told the states apart with over 95 per cent accuracy, against about 63 per cent for standard brain-wave power. The authors call the work exploratory: one session, separate groups, four channels.
- Singh, Beuria, Behera, Pachori and Gupta (2026) combined EEG with heart-rate variability and trained deep neural networks to classify before, during and after phases of a task. The networks found it *harder* to tell the phases apart in the mantra group than in the control group, which the authors read as mantra lowering cognitive load. That is an indirect inference from a drop in classifier accuracy, and I could not find the sample size in the published abstract.
- Sharma and colleagues (2026), in a short conference chapter, asked novices chanting the mahāmantra whether they were attentive or distracted, and found more slow delta activity when distracted and more fast occipital gamma when attentive. Again, no sample size in the abstract and no replication yet.

These are methods papers. They show that mantra practice leaves a pattern in the EEG that a well-built classifier can pick up, which is worth knowing. They do not show that the pattern is good for you, or that it is specific to mantra rather than to sitting still and doing any repetitive verbal task. I grade them suggestive as methods and speculative as evidence of benefit.

The Transcendental Meditation literature has the longest EEG record, centred on frontal alpha coherence. The randomised study most often cited, Travis and colleagues in 2009, assigned 50 students to start TM immediately or after ten weeks and reported improvements on a "Brain Integration Scale". But the comparison was a waiting list, a quarter of the data were lost, the scale is the research group's own composite, and most of the authors are affiliated with the TM organisation. Suggestive, with a conflict of interest that should be stated every time it is cited.

## Where I land

| Claim | Grade | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mantra practice modestly reduces anxiety, depression and stress | **E** | 51 studies pooled; effects small-to-moderate |
| It does so more than other meditation or relaxation | **X** | Not shown; against relaxation, no significant difference |
| Repeating a word quiets the mind-wandering (default mode) network | **S** | One fMRI study of 23 people, consistent with the meta-analysis |
| Chanting OM calms limbic regions | **S** | Pilot of 12, no replication |
| Mantra moments show more theta and alpha than mind-wandering moments, in experienced practitioners | **S** | 24 people, strong within-person design |
| EEG can classify mantra states from other states | **S** | Several small exploratory studies |
| A specific sacred mantra does something a neutral word cannot | **X** | One small 1994 study hints at a difference; never replicated |

The last row is the one I would most like to see tested properly. The tradition is clear that the mantra matters — that *oṃ* or a name of God is not interchangeable with the word "one". The clearest brain study used the word "one" and still found the quieting effect.

The closest test I have found is old and small. In 1994 Telles, Nagarathna and Nagendra recorded the brain's early response to clicks in 18 men, nine with more than ten years of OM meditation and nine beginners, once while silently repeating "one" and once while meditating on OM. In the experienced group, one component of the response grew during OM and shrank during "one". The authors' own reading was careful: both conditions changed the brain at the same level, possibly in opposite directions. Eighteen people, one measure, all men, and no replication I can find. It is a reason to run the experiment again with modern methods, not an answer.

Until someone does, the fair summary is this. A mantra gives attention something simple to do, and that seems to turn down the part of the brain that would otherwise wander. That is a real effect and a good reason to practise. Whether the word itself carries more than that is a question the tradition answers and the research has barely begun to ask.
