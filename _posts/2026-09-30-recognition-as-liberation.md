## In brief

This article argues that Abhinavagupta's non-dualism is best read as a philosophy of recognition rather than a philosophy of negation. Where some Indian non-dual systems treat the plural world as something to be seen through and set aside, the Trika Śaivism of tenth- and eleventh-century Kashmir treats that same world as the self-display of a conscious and free absolute. The study reconstructs four linked ideas: the paired notions of *prakāśa* (manifesting light) and *vimarśa* (reflexive self-awareness); the doctrine of *svātantrya* (sovereign freedom) as the source of manifestation; the account of limitation through the *tattvas*, *kañcukas* and *malas*; and the soteriology of *pratyabhijñā*, in which liberation is the recognition of an identity that was never lost. A further section examines how Abhinavagupta carried this model into aesthetics through his theory of *rasa*. The article closes by comparing the Trika position with Śaṅkara's Advaita Vedānta and by weighing what the tradition can and cannot offer to present-day discussions of consciousness.

## Introduction

Any philosophy that claims reality is "one" must eventually explain why experience so stubbornly presents itself as many. We meet trees, other people, our own shifting moods, the passage of time. A non-dualist has broadly two options. The first is to say that plurality is ultimately unreal, a misperception layered over a single featureless ground. The second is to say that plurality is real as an expression of the one ground, so that difference appears without ever amounting to a second, independent reality.

Abhinavagupta (fl. c. 975–1025 CE) took the second road, and he took it with unusual rigour. Working in Kashmir as a philosopher, exegete, ritual theorist and literary critic, he drew together several streams of Śaiva thought into a synthesis usually called Trika or, in its more philosophical register, the Pratyabhijñā ("Recognition") school. His central claim can be put simply: the absolute, named Śiva, is consciousness (*cit*), and this consciousness is not inert but free, self-aware and creative. The universe is what that freedom looks like when it expresses itself.

This article pursues three questions. First, what conception of consciousness allows Abhinavagupta to affirm both unity and genuine multiplicity? Second, if everything is already Śiva, what exactly is bondage, and what does liberation change? Third, how does this metaphysics extend into his celebrated theory of aesthetic experience? The discussion relies chiefly on the Śiva Sūtras, Utpaladeva's Īśvarapratyabhijñākārikā, the Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra, and Abhinavagupta's own Tantrāloka, Tantrasāra, Īśvarapratyabhijñāvimarśinī and Abhinavabhāratī, read alongside modern scholarship.

## Historical and textual context

The non-dual Śaivism of Kashmir did not begin with Abhinavagupta. Its scriptural base lies in a body of Tantras, among them the Mālinīvijayottara, which Abhinavagupta treated as the authoritative root of Trika. In the ninth century, according to tradition, the sage Vasugupta received the Śiva Sūtras, a compact collection of aphorisms on consciousness and liberation. His disciple Kallaṭa is credited with the Spanda Kārikās, which develop the idea of consciousness as a dynamic "pulsation" (*spanda*).

The decisive philosophical turn came with Somānanda's Śivadṛṣṭi and, above all, with his student Utpaladeva, whose Īśvarapratyabhijñākārikā gave the tradition a systematic argument. Utpaladeva engaged directly with Buddhist epistemologists such as Dharmakīrti, who denied any enduring self. Against them he argued that memory, synthesis of perceptions and the unity of experience all require a persisting, self-aware subject.

Abhinavagupta stood in the third generation of this lineage, having studied with Utpaladeva's student Lakṣmaṇagupta. His achievement was twofold. In the Īśvarapratyabhijñāvimarśinī and its longer companion commentary, he refined the Pratyabhijñā arguments. In the Tantrāloka, a vast compendium, and its digest the Tantrasāra, he integrated that philosophy with ritual, yoga and cosmology. His student Kṣemarāja later produced the Pratyabhijñāhṛdaya, a short and widely read primer that popularised many of the formulations discussed below.

## Prakāśa and vimarśa: the structure of consciousness

The Trika analysis starts from a modest observation. Whatever we encounter, whether a colour, a melody, a memory or a flash of irritation, shares one feature: it is manifest. It shows up. The tradition names this manifesting quality *prakāśa*, literally "light" or "shining." An object that did not appear to any awareness could not even be spoken of, so *prakāśa* is treated as the condition of possibility for anything at all being present.

Had the analysis stopped there, the result might resemble a pure witness-consciousness, luminous but passive, much like a mirror. Abhinavagupta insists that this picture is incomplete. A mirror reflects objects but does not know that it reflects them. Consciousness, by contrast, is aware of itself as aware. This reflexive grasp is *vimarśa*, variously rendered as self-reflection, self-apprehension or active awareness.

The pairing matters because *vimarśa* is what makes consciousness a subject rather than a mere field. It also grounds language, judgement and will, since *vimarśa* is closely tied to the tradition's concept of *parāvāk*, the supreme level of speech. Without *vimarśa*, Abhinavagupta argues, consciousness would be indistinguishable from an insentient object such as a crystal. The absolute is therefore not a static being but a self-knowing activity. Kṣemarāja captures the point when he describes ultimate reality as a union of light and reflective awareness in which neither can exist without the other.

The Śiva Sūtras compress this into their opening aphorism, *caitanyam ātmā*, "consciousness is the Self" (ŚS 1.1). The term *caitanya* here denotes not a passive glow but the full freedom of knowing and acting. The self in question is not the body or the personality. It is the reflexive awareness that knows both.

## Svātantrya and ābhāsa: how the world appears

If consciousness is the only reality, the obvious puzzle is how a world of apparently separate things arises. Abhinavagupta's answer rests on *svātantrya*, the sovereign freedom of consciousness. Because Śiva is self-aware, Śiva is also free, and freedom in this system means above all the capacity to manifest. The universe is not produced out of some external material, nor does it emerge by accident. Consciousness displays itself, within itself, as the whole range of subjects and objects.

The technical term for such a manifestation is *ābhāsa*, an "appearance" or "shining forth." The word can mislead a reader familiar with Advaita Vedānta, where appearance often suggests illusion. In Trika usage, an *ābhāsa* is real precisely as an expression of consciousness. The pot on the table is not a false image concealing a true Brahman. It is Śiva appearing as a pot. What would be false is the belief that the pot exists on its own, cut off from the awareness in which it shines.

An image the tradition itself favours is the mirror. Reflections in a mirror are many and varied, yet none of them is anything other than the mirror. The difference is that in the Trika case the mirror also produces its own reflections through its own will. The frequently cited ocean-and-wave comparison makes a similar point: waves differ from one another without ever being separate from water.

This model dissolves the usual tension between transcendence and immanence. Śiva exceeds every particular appearance, since no finite form exhausts infinite freedom. Yet Śiva is also wholly present in each appearance, since each is nothing but Śiva's self-expression. Kṣemarāja expresses this with the phrase that consciousness, of its own free will, unfolds the universe on its own screen.

## Contraction: tattvas, kañcukas and malas

A philosophy that makes everything divine must still explain ordinary human limitation. Trika does so through the idea of *saṅkoca*, self-contraction. The infinite does not become deficient. It freely assumes the appearance of limits, much as a light seen through a narrow opening appears small without the light itself shrinking.

### The thirty-six tattvas

The tradition maps this contraction as a sequence of thirty-six *tattvas*, or principles of reality. The scheme absorbs the twenty-five categories of Sāṃkhya and places above them a set of higher, "pure" principles.

| Level | Tattvas | What they describe |
|---|---|---|
| Pure order (1–5) | Śiva, Śakti, Sadāśiva, Īśvara, Śuddhavidyā | Consciousness in full self-possession, with a growing sense of "I" and "this" still held in unity |
| Māyā and its sheaths (6–11) | Māyā, kalā, vidyā, rāga, kāla, niyati | The veiling power that introduces difference and the five limitations of the individual |
| Subject and nature (12–13) | Puruṣa, Prakṛti | The limited experiencer and the matrix of objects |
| Inner instrument (14–16) | Buddhi, ahaṃkāra, manas | Intellect, ego-sense and mind |
| Senses and elements (17–36) | Five cognitive senses, five organs of action, five subtle elements, five gross elements | The field of embodied perception and matter |

The list is not a moral ladder from good to bad. It traces how a single consciousness progressively takes on narrower forms, and it can be read in reverse as a map of return.

### The five kañcukas

Māyā operates through five "sheaths" (*kañcukas*), each of which restricts one of Śiva's universal qualities. *Kalā* narrows omnipotence into limited agency. *Vidyā* narrows omniscience into partial knowledge. *Rāga* turns fullness into a sense of lack that shows up as desire. *Kāla* subjects the timeless to sequence. *Niyati* binds the free to fixed causes and places.

### The three malas

Alongside the sheaths stand three "impurities" (*malas*). *Āṇava mala* is the root sense of being small and incomplete. *Māyīya mala* is the perception of the world as fundamentally divided. *Kārma mala* is identification with one's actions and their results. These terms should not be read as sins in a moral sense. They name structural features of finite experience.

On this reading, the Śiva Sūtras' second aphorism, *jñānaṃ bandhaḥ* (ŚS 1.2), becomes clear. It does not condemn knowledge as such. It says that knowledge under contraction, the knowing that says "I am only this and not that," is what binds.

## Pratyabhijñā: liberation as recognition

The school's name supplies its soteriology. *Pratyabhijñā* is the ordinary Sanskrit word for recognising someone or something already known. Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta illustrate it with the case of a woman who has heard of a man's virtues and longs for him, yet feels nothing when he stands before her, until someone tells her who he is. At that moment nothing new is added to the man. What changes is her grasp of what was already present.

Liberation works the same way. The individual does not become Śiva by acquiring a new substance or climbing out of the world. The individual recognises that the awareness it has always been is Śiva. This is why the tradition can describe the goal as already accomplished and still require a real transformation. The transformation is cognitive and existential, not ontological.

The analysis of the word *aham* ("I") is central here. In everyday use, "I" is attached to passing descriptions: tired, clever, wronged, successful. These predicates change constantly, while the sense of being a subject persists through them. Abhinavagupta calls the unrestricted form of this subjectivity *pūrṇāhantā*, "perfect I-ness." The limited ego is not an enemy to be destroyed. It is a contracted version of that universal "I," and liberation consists in the contraction being seen for what it is.

Two consequences follow. First, the world need not be renounced, since the problem was never the existence of objects but the misreading of them as independent. Second, liberation is not a special mental state that comes and goes. Trance, silence and bliss are all states, and states pass. What Trika calls recognition is an abiding understanding of the ground in which every state appears. A verse of the Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra (v. 116) puts the practical implication vividly: wherever the mind goes, outward or inward, the condition of Śiva is found there, because it pervades everything.

## Means and grace: upāyas, śaktipāta and the paradox of practice

A doctrine of recognition invites an objection. If one is already Śiva, why practise anything? Abhinavagupta's reply is that knowing a proposition is not the same as realising it. A scholar may repeat "consciousness is the Self" flawlessly and still live wholly identified with body and circumstance. Practice does not manufacture the goal. It removes whatever obscures recognition.

In the Tantrāloka he arranges practice into graded "means" (*upāyas*), matched to the ripeness of the practitioner:

| Upāya | Sphere of work | Typical methods |
|---|---|---|
| Āṇavopāya | The individual's body, breath and objects | Breath awareness, concentration, visualisation, mantra recitation, ritual |
| Śāktopāya | Thought and cognition | Refining concepts until they point beyond themselves, contemplative reasoning, subtle mantra |
| Śāmbhavopāya | Will and pure awareness | Turning attention directly to the source of awareness, with minimal support |
| Anupāya | No means | Spontaneous recognition, for one already fully ripe |

The Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra shows what these means look like in detail. Its 112 contemplations use the pause between breaths, the fading edge of a sound, the open sky, intense joy or fear, and even sneezing or hunger as openings. Its logic is consistent. Ordinary attention is swallowed by the contents of experience, but at moments of transition or intensity that absorption briefly loosens, and the awareness behind it can be noticed. The instruction is not to indulge each impulse. It is to notice the knowing in which the impulse appears.

Above every method stands grace. The five acts of Śiva (*pañcakṛtya*) are emission, maintenance, withdrawal, concealment and grace (*anugraha*). Concealment explains why beings forget their nature. Grace, often experienced as *śaktipāta*, the "descent of power," explains why they begin to remember. Abhinavagupta does not treat grace and effort as rivals, because both occur within the one freedom. The seeker's search is itself an expression of the reality being sought.

## Aesthetics: rasa and universalisation

Abhinavagupta's influence reaches far beyond religious philosophy because he carried his model of consciousness into literary theory. In the Abhinavabhāratī, his commentary on Bharata's Nāṭyaśāstra, and the Locana, his commentary on Ānandavardhana's Dhvanyāloka, he addresses an old question: what exactly does an audience enjoy when it watches a tragedy?

Earlier critics had proposed that the emotion is located in the original character, or that the actor imitates it, or that the spectator infers it. Building on the work of Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka, Abhinavagupta argues that none of these explains the peculiar quality of aesthetic experience. Grief on stage does not make us want to flee, as real grief would. Instead we savour it.

His explanation is *sādhāraṇīkaraṇa*, "generalisation" or "universalisation." Through the conventions of drama and poetry, an emotion is lifted free of its particular owner. The sorrow is neither the character's alone nor the spectator's private sorrow. It is sorrow as such, experienced by a mind that has temporarily set aside the anxious question "what does this mean for me?" The resulting relish is *rasa*.

The link to his metaphysics is direct. In *rasa*, the grip of the limited ego loosens and consciousness tastes its own content without the usual contraction. Abhinavagupta therefore describes aesthetic rapture as akin to the bliss of recognising the absolute. He is careful not to identify them. Aesthetic delight is temporary and depends on the performance, while liberation is lasting. Yet the resemblance is instructive, and it gives Trika a way to explain why art can feel like a glimpse of something larger than oneself. He also gave special standing to *śānta rasa*, the rasa of tranquillity, as the one most closely aligned with liberation.

## Discussion

### Trika and Advaita Vedānta compared

Both Śaṅkara's Advaita Vedānta and Abhinavagupta's Trika affirm a single conscious reality, and both locate human bondage in ignorance of it. Their disagreement lies in how they value the world.

| Question | Advaita Vedānta (Śaṅkara) | Trika Śaivism (Abhinavagupta) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of the absolute | Brahman as pure, actionless consciousness | Śiva as consciousness endowed with freedom and self-awareness |
| Status of the world | Neither real nor unreal (*anirvacanīya*); an appearance due to ignorance | A real manifestation (*ābhāsa*) of consciousness |
| Role of māyā | Obscuring power bound up with ignorance | A power of Śiva that Śiva freely wields |
| Agency | Belongs to the empirical level only | Belongs to the absolute itself |
| Goal | Knowledge that sublates the world | Recognition that sees the world as Śiva |

For the Trika critic, an absolute lacking *vimarśa* and agency cannot account for why anything appears at all. For the Advaitin, attributing activity to the absolute risks importing change into what must be changeless. The debate is genuine, and neither side can be declared the winner by a simple comparison.

### Contemporary relevance

Abhinavagupta's thought has attracted interest from philosophers of mind and scholars of contemplative practice. His insistence that awareness is reflexive, that consciousness knows itself in knowing anything, has parallels in current debates about pre-reflective self-awareness in phenomenology. His contemplative methods also resonate with attention-based practices used in some therapeutic settings. His aesthetics has found a place in comparative literary theory as an early and sophisticated account of how art depersonalises emotion.

### Limits of the analysis

Some caution is needed. Trika was an initiatory tradition embedded in ritual, lineage and specific Tantric scriptures. Presenting it purely as a philosophy of awareness, as many modern popularisations do, strips away much of what its authors thought essential. Popular sources also frequently misattribute verses, for example crediting lines from the Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra to the Spanda Kārikās, or quoting passages of uncertain origin as Utpaladeva's. Serious study should rely on critical editions and scholarly translations. Finally, the metaphysical claim that the universe is a manifestation of consciousness remains a philosophical thesis, not an empirical finding, and it should be evaluated as such.

## Conclusion

Abhinavagupta's non-dualism rests on a single, carefully argued conviction: consciousness is not only the light in which things appear but a free, self-aware power that appears as those things. From this conviction follow the rest of his positions. The world is real as manifestation rather than false as illusion. Bondage is self-contraction rather than a stain added from outside. Liberation is recognition rather than escape. Even the pleasure of art becomes evidence of what consciousness is like when its ordinary narrowness relaxes.

The result is a non-dualism that affirms embodied life instead of retreating from it. Its enduring interest lies less in any one doctrine than in the question it keeps returning to. When everything that can be observed has been set aside as an object, what remains is the observing itself. For Abhinavagupta, to see that clearly, and to see the world as its expression, is what it means to recognise Śiva.
