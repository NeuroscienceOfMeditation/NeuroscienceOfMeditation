The hands tingle first, usually. Then the lips. Then a lightness in the head, a sense that the edges of the body have gone soft, sometimes heat moving up the spine, sometimes crying that arrives without a reason attached to it.

I have felt all of that. I have been in rooms where it was happening to twenty people at once. And I have heard it explained, every time, in the same vocabulary: energy is moving, a blockage is clearing, prana is rising.

I am not going to tell you the experience was not real. It was real. I am going to tell you what else was happening in those bodies at the same time, because somebody has now measured it, and the number is startling.

## Carbon dioxide is not waste

The first thing I had wrong for years is that I thought of breathing as taking oxygen in. Oxygen is the glamorous half. Carbon dioxide is the half that does the regulating.

Dissolved CO₂ sets the acidity of your blood. Breathe it off faster than your body makes it and blood pH rises — respiratory alkalosis. Your brain's arterioles also read CO₂ directly, and they read it as an instruction about how much blood to let through.

So the question "what did that breathing practice do to me" has a boring, measurable first answer before it has an interesting one.

Normal is around 35 to 36 mmHg of CO₂ in the breath you exhale. I am not quoting a textbook range at you — that is roughly what the baseline readings look like in the two studies I am about to describe, in ordinary people sitting quietly before anything happened.

## The number that changed my mind

In April 2025, Havenith and colleagues published a study in *Communications Psychology* that I think every breathwork teacher should read.

They took sixty-one experienced breathwork practitioners through five group sessions — two of Holotropic Breathwork lasting about three hours, three of Conscious-Connected Breathwork lasting about ninety minutes. Participants were randomly assigned, roughly seventy-thirty, either to do the active breathing or to sit through the same session breathing normally. Forty-three active, eighteen passive. Throughout, the researchers measured the carbon dioxide in each person's exhaled breath, and asked them at six points how deep the experience had become.

The active breathers' carbon dioxide bottomed out at **16.6 mmHg**. The people sitting in the same room breathing normally sat at 34.3.

Half. Not a nudge, not a drift — half the normal value, sustained.

And the depth of the experience tracked it. Average CO₂ and average reported depth correlated at r = −0.46 in the Holotropic sessions (p = 0.012) and r = −0.47 in the Conscious-Connected ones (p = 0.008). The lower the carbon dioxide, the deeper the state. On the standard altered-states questionnaires the active breathers scored in the range reported for psilocybin, and well above placebo.

I want to be careful here, because this is the kind of result it is easy to oversell. It is a correlation, not a demonstration of cause. The study was exploratory and not preregistered. The passive group was eighteen people. Follow-up data existed for only twenty-five of the sixty-one. And the comparison with psilocybin used reference scores from a database rather than a drug arm run alongside it.

But the direction is not ambiguous, and the magnitude is not small.

## What that does to the blood supply of the brain

Low CO₂ constricts cerebral arterioles. The figure usually given is around a 3 per cent fall in cerebral blood flow for each 1 mmHg fall in arterial CO₂, with the vessels responding across roughly the 20 to 60 mmHg range — Bogossian and colleagues state it plainly in a 2021 review in *Frontiers in Neurology*, written for intensive care rather than for yoga.

I am not going to do the arithmetic on that and hand you a percentage, because the CO₂ in your exhaled breath is not the same measurement as the CO₂ in your artery, and the two come apart exactly when breathing is abnormal. But the direction holds, and it is not trivial.

The nearest measurement in a yogic practice is one I have cited before. Nivethitha and colleagues put transcranial Doppler on fifteen healthy practitioners and watched the middle cerebral artery during a minute of *bhastrika* — forceful bellows breathing — and a minute of breath retention. Forceful breathing **lowered** mean flow velocity by 22.0. Retention raised it by 43.7, about twice as far in the other direction.

## The symptom list already exists, in a literature nobody in a breathwork room is reading

Here is the part I found hardest to look at.

Pfortmueller and colleagues reviewed 616 adults who arrived at a Swiss emergency department over six years with primary hyperventilation — people who were breathing too much, for no cardiac or pulmonary reason. Their paper is in *PLOS ONE*, 2015.

Of the 244 who had blood gases taken, mean pH was 7.47 and mean CO₂ was 29.76 mmHg, with one reading as low as 12.

And this is what those people reported.

## Mechanism and measurement are different things

The usual account of why alkalosis makes you tingle is that raising blood pH increases the amount of calcium bound to albumin, lowering the free ionised calcium, which makes nerve membranes fire more readily — hence pins and needles first in the extremities and around the mouth, and in extreme cases cramping of the hands and feet.

I want to flag what that sentence is. It is the standard textbook mechanism, and it is plausible, and it is not what the emergency department study measured. What that study measured is an association: these people had low CO₂ and alkaline blood, and this is the proportion who felt these things. Treat the mechanism as the explanation on offer, not as a result.

## None of this is an argument against breathwork

Two things get collapsed together and they should not be.

**Slow breathing** — lengthening the breath, lowering the rate, nothing forceful — is one of the better-evidenced interventions in the whole field. Zaccaro and colleagues reviewed it in *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* in 2018, and I have written here before about the autonomic evidence. That is not the practice described above.

**Fast or forceful breathing** sustained for twenty minutes is a different physiological event with a different mechanism and a different risk profile.

And it is worth saying plainly that the Havenith study is not a debunking. Those participants reported real changes, the depth of the experience predicted improved well-being and reduced depressive symptoms afterwards, and the authors treat it as a possible non-pharmacological route to therapeutically useful states. The state may well be worth having.

What I am questioning is only the explanation attached to it in the room.

## Even the gentle version does this a little

This is the finding I did not expect, and it is the practical one.

Szulczewski, writing in *Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback* in 2019, had forty-six young adults breathe at 0.1 Hz for ten minutes — four seconds in, six seconds out. That is the standard slow-breathing pace, the one in every coherent-breathing app, the one I teach.

Without any further instruction, their exhaled CO₂ fell from 36.17 to 30.96 mmHg, and their self-reported hyperventilation symptoms rose measurably.

A second group got one extra instruction before they started: keep the breath shallow, do not breathe deeply. Their CO₂ fell by only 2.7 mmHg instead of 5.2, and their symptoms did not move at all.

I had been giving half of that instruction for years. Slow down, I would say. I did not say: and keep it small. On this evidence those are two different instructions, and leaving the second one out is why people get light-headed doing a practice that is supposed to settle them.

## What I actually do with this now

When tingling, light-headedness or a dissolving sense of the body's edges arrives during fast or deep breathing, I now treat it as a carbon dioxide reading before I treat it as anything else. Not because the experience is fake, but because a sensation that appears reliably when CO₂ drops and disappears reliably when it does not is a sensation with a known input. "Prana rising" has to do work that "my CO₂ is at sixteen" is already doing.

Three practical notes.

Slow the breath without deepening it. That is the whole of the Szulczewski finding and it costs nothing.

If you want to know, measure. A capnometer is not expensive, and it turns "was that energy?" into a question with an answer. That is the sort of thing this site exists for.

And if forceful breathing produces cramping in the hands or feet, fainting, or a sense of unreality that does not clear when you stop, that is a signal to stop, not a milestone. If you have a heart condition, epilepsy, or a history of panic, whether to do intensive breathwork at all is a conversation with a doctor, not something a web page can settle for you.
