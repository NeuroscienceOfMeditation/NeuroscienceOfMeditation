Last week I wrote that lying on one side opens the other nostril, and that this is the best-supported instruction in the whole swara yoga toolkit. I was pleased with that piece. It took me a few days to notice what it does to everything else I have written about the breath.

If posture steers which nostril is open, and people spend all day and all night changing posture, then some share of what has been recorded as an endogenous rhythm is a record of how the person was sitting and lying. Not a rhythm in the body. A rhythm in the furniture.

I would like to say I spotted that. I did not. Somebody put it in a title thirty-seven years ago, and then went and answered it.

## Somebody answered this in 1989

In *The Laryngoscope* in May 1989, James Haight and Philip Cole published four pages titled "Is the Nasal Cycle an Artifact? The Role of Asymmetrical Postures."

They had spent years documenting that posture changes nasal resistance. Then they turned round and asked, in print, whether the rhythm they had been measuring was partly their own subjects lying on things. I find that admirable. It is the question you ask when you would rather be right than be first.

Their design is the clever part, and it is also the whole limitation.

The posture response is close to universal. They had tested forty-two patients for it. Five did not respond on first testing, and one of those failed to respond despite being tested on numerous occasions. Her nose, in other words, ignored posture. So they took that one woman — twenty-two years old, clinically normal nose — and used her as a natural knockout. If the cycle is manufactured by postural pressure, a person in whom postural pressure does nothing should not have one.

The control came first. She lay in a head-out body plethysmograph on a six-inch mattress, on the side of her more open nostril, for ten to ninety minutes, on eleven separate days. No phase reversal. The lever that works on almost everybody did not work on her.

Then the experiment. Two days sitting in the plethysmograph for six to seven hours, and a third lying flat on her back for five, with postural asymmetry minimised throughout. Posterior rhinometry recording each side.

The cycle appeared anyway. Phase reversals in both postures.

They put two more observations behind it. Standing people show a nasal cycle, but standing on one foot does not change nasal resistance. And the cycle continues through prolonged lateral recumbency, when the small shifts a person makes do not alter the pressure difference between the two halves of the body.

Their conclusion: the cycle is not the result of asymmetrical body pressures.

I think that is the right answer, and I want to be clear about what it rests on. It is one subject. And she was selected precisely for being abnormal in the dimension under test — the single person out of forty-two whose nose would not do the thing everybody else's nose does. A reflex that is missing in one person might be missing alongside other things. The design is elegant and the sample is a sample of one, and both of those are true at once.

So the artefact question has an answer, and the answer is no. What it does not have is a measurement.

## What posture is still doing to the data

Saying the cycle is not *caused* by posture is not the same as saying posture is not *in* the recordings. Haight and Cole are explicit about this in their own discussion: the posture reflex is real, it is elicited by lateral recumbency, by pressure to the chest wall or shoulder, and by sitting on one side, and the changes it produces "resemble the nasal cycle." They note that such postural adjustments are bound to happen over long recordings, particularly when subjects have not been warned against them.

That is the live problem, and it is not the one their study closes. Their subject could not produce a posture response. Forty-one of the forty-two people they tested could, and so, presumably, can almost everybody in every other recording ever made.

## The one study that watched both at once

Rohrmeier and colleagues, in *The Laryngoscope* in 2014, recorded twenty healthy people for about twenty-three hours each with long-term rhinoflowmetry, and — this is the part that matters — tracked head and body position through the night at the same time.

Two findings set the scene. A classic alternating cycle appeared in 50 per cent of their subjects while awake and 75 per cent while asleep. And once people were lying down, body position correlated with which side was congested, r = 0.67, p = 0.024.

Then they did the thing almost nobody does: they looked at the order of events.

Positional shifts led to a subsequent change in which nostril was dominant in 22 per cent of cases, p < 0.01. Run the test the other way — did a change in dominance lead to a shift in body position — and the figure was 30.3 per cent, p = 0.16. Not significant.

I want to be careful about how much that carries. Twenty people is small, and a difference in whether two numbers clear a significance threshold is not the same as a difference between the two numbers. But the direction of the authors' own conclusion is unambiguous, and it is the uncomfortable one: your body moves your nose more than your nose moves your body.

## The modern check, and the window it missed

The largest dataset in this area is Kahana-Zweig and colleagues in *PLoS ONE* in 2016 — thirty-three adults, twenty-four hours of continuous dual-nostril airflow logging each, with a waist-mounted accelerometer recording position throughout.

They found the posture effect, clearly. Mean laterality was 0.21 lying on the left and −0.29 lying on the right, across nine position categories, F(8,168) = 7.01, p < 0.0001, with the left-versus-right contrast the only significant pairwise comparison, p < 0.002.

And then, to their credit, they checked whether that effect was manufacturing the cycle. They compared laterality amplitude in the five minutes before each position change against the five minutes after, pooled across twenty-three subjects. No difference: t(165) = 0.4, p = 0.68. Changing position did not, by itself, throw a false switch into the record.

That is a real result and it rules out a real worry. It also tests the wrong window.

Those are two different claims. One is that rolling over produces a transient in the trace. The other is that sustained asymmetric pressure, held over the hours that a dominance phase actually lasts, sets which side leads. The first has been tested and cleared. The second has not been tested at all, and it is the one that matters, because it is the one that describes how people actually lie: not flipping every few minutes, but settling into a side and staying there.

## Three more things posture cannot account for

Beyond the 1989 knockout, the evidence against a pure artefact comes from three directions.

The first is sleep stage. Frye, Rosin, Morrison, Leon-Sarmiento and Doty recorded six adults through an eight-hour sleep period in *Arquivos de Neuro-Psiquiatria* in 2017, with nasal thermistors, full polysomnography and infrared video tracking body position. Airflow was proportionally greater through the right nostril during REM sleep than non-REM, p < 0.001. Position mattered too, p < 0.001, and the two interacted. But sleep stage is not posture, and it moved the nose. Six people is very few. The direction is still a datum posture cannot supply.

That paper also makes a suggestion I think is the most useful methodological line in this whole literature: supine airflow may be the best window onto the endogenous cycle, because lying flat is the one position with no asymmetric pressure in it.

The second is that the cycle has a plausible central machine behind it. Williams and Eccles, in the *Journal of Laryngology and Otology* in 2016, recorded thirty subjects by rhinomanometry over seven-hour sessions on two days about a week apart, and found reciprocal changes in fifty of sixty recordings, with correlations running from −0.89 to +0.97. They propose a hypothalamic centre driving two brainstem half-centres. Worth noticing that ten of those sixty recordings were in-phase rather than reciprocal; even the central model has to make room for days when the two sides do not trade off.

The third is the strangest, and I am going to label it clearly as what it is. Bremner and Gälldin Nordström reported in *Neuro-Ophthalmology* in 2017 on one man, aged thirty-four, with alternating anisocoria — pupils that take turns being the larger one. Over twenty-one days of daily webcam recordings and breath-on-mirror tests, the larger pupil was always on the same side as the more open nostril. No discordant readings. They suggest a shared central oscillator modulating sympathetic outflow to both the nasal vessels and the iris.

Your pupils do not know which side you slept on. If that concordance holds up, it is hard to explain with furniture. It is also a single case report, the authors say so themselves, and nobody has tested it in a group. I include it because it is the cleanest conceptual evidence for a central pendulum that exists, and because the fact that the cleanest evidence is one man with a webcam tells you how thin this field is.

## And a dull mechanism that is not the cycle at all

Worth keeping separate from all of this: posture changes the nose in a boring way too. Pendolino, Lund, Nardello and Ottaviano, reviewing the field in *Rhinology Online* in 2018, report that moving from sitting to supine raises central venous pressure by up to 8 mmHg, which congests the nose regardless of which side the cycle happens to be favouring. That is not a cycle effect and not a laterality effect. It just means that if you measure your breath lying down and compare it to a measurement taken sitting up, you are not comparing like with like.

Their review, incidentally, does not treat the cycle as an artefact either. It treats posture as something that overrides the cycle temporarily and then lets it resume, phase reversed, once the position is held — the corporo-nasal reflex Haight and Cole had described three years before they went looking for its limits.

## A footnote I did not expect in a 1989 rhinology paper

In the discussion, explaining where the axillary pressure idea comes from, Haight and Cole write that the writers of the Yoga literature noted that lateral recumbency, or the unilateral application of pressure by a crutch under the arm, causes obstruction on that side and improvement on the other. They add, flatly, that the response is well documented.

Their reference list for that sentence includes Bhole and Karambelkar in *Yoga Mimamsa* in 1968, sitting between papers on sympathetic stimulation in cats and rheology of nasal airflow. Elsewhere on the same page they date the scientific documentation of the cycle to Kayser in 1895 and say the alternation "was discussed in the ancient Yoga literature."

I did not expect to find the *yogadaṇḍa* in the bibliography of the paper that settles whether the nasal cycle is real. It does not make the tradition right about what the breath means. It is a reminder that the observational part of it was good enough to be cited by people measuring the same thing with a plethysmograph ninety years later.

## Where I land

The question I started with was whether the nasal cycle is really a rhythm or really a record of posture. That question has an answer and the answer is no, it is not an artefact. One woman who could not produce a posture response cycled anyway, in two postures, across three recording days. Standing people cycle, and standing on one foot does nothing. Sleep stage moves the nose and sleep stage is not furniture. A central model fits, and the pupil case, for what one case is worth, points the same way.

What does not have an answer is the quantity. Posture is a real input in almost everybody — forty-one of the forty-two patients Haight and Cole tested had the reflex — and it acts on exactly the variable the recordings measure. The one modern test of whether it contaminates the traces looked at ten minutes either side of rolling over, when the window that matters is a dominance phase averaging over two hours.

So the honest statement is narrower than either headline. The cycle is real and it is not made of mattresses. The amplitude and the regularity in published group traces are probably flattered by posture, by an amount nobody has measured. The study that would measure it is not hard to describe: record dominance continuously in a decent-sized group while holding posture constant — supine, or seated upright, across several dominance spells — and see what survives. Frye and colleagues' supine data is the nearest anyone has come, and it is six people over one night.

## Two things I am changing

The first is in the tool. The Swar Yoga page asks you to observe which nostril is open and then compares that against what the text expects. If you check straight after lying on one side, you are not reading your cycle, you are reading your bed. The observation step needs to say: sit upright for a few minutes first, then check. I will put that in.

The second is a correction to myself. Last week I presented the posture finding as a win for the tradition — the text says you can change which nostril is flowing, and you can. That was true, and I wrote it as though it cost nothing. It costs something. The better the lever works, the worse the oracle looks, because a schedule set by the lunar day has to compete with a schedule set by which side you slept on. The tradition found the lever. It never mentions the competitor.

I think that is the right way round to be wrong, and I would rather say it here than quietly not mention it.
